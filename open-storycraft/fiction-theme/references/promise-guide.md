# The Reader Promise Guide

## What the Promise Is

The reader promise is the implicit contract formed in the first pages of a novel. It tells the reader: this is the kind of experience you're going to have. It covers tone, genre, stakes, and the type of satisfaction the ending will deliver.

Every novel makes a promise. The question is whether the author makes it intentionally.

## The Promise Statement Formula

A strong promise can be distilled to one sentence:

> "This is a story about **[character type]** who **[faces what]** and discovers **[thematic truth]** at the cost of **[sacrifice]**."

### Breaking Down the Components

**Character type**: Not a name. A descriptor that tells the reader who they're investing in.
- "a twenty-year-old captain who never wanted the job"
- "a retired detective with nothing left to lose"
- "a girl who can hear the dead but can't tell anyone"

**Faces what**: The central conflict in concrete terms.
- "must lead a crew through enemy territory"
- "is pulled back into a case that destroyed his family"
- "discovers the dead are trying to warn her about the living"

**Thematic truth**: What the story reveals about life, identity, or human nature.
- "that leadership means choosing who you can't save"
- "that some cases solve you"
- "that the dead can't protect you from the choices you make"

**Sacrifice**: What the protagonist gives up. This is what makes the story matter.
- "his belief that he can protect everyone"
- "the peaceful retirement he built to forget"
- "the safety of silence"

### Examples

> "This is a story about a farm boy who discovers he's the heir to a broken throne and learns that the right to rule must be earned through sacrifice, not blood, at the cost of the simple life he loved."

> "This is a story about two rival chefs forced to share a kitchen who discover that vulnerability is the secret ingredient in everything worth making, at the cost of the armor they've spent their whole careers building."

> "This is a story about a detective in a city that forgot how to sleep who discovers that the conspiracy goes higher than justice can reach, at the cost of believing that truth is enough."

## What the Opening Pages Promise

The first three chapters are the promise contract. Whatever you establish there, you must deliver.

### Tone Promise
If the opening is funny, the book must be funny. If the opening is dark, the book must be dark. Tonal whiplash between Act 1 and Act 3 is a betrayal.

**This doesn't mean the tone can't deepen.** A comedy can get serious. A thriller can have moments of warmth. But the fundamental register must hold. A reader who signed up for a romp will feel betrayed by a tragedy.

### Genre Promise
The first pages establish which genre conventions are in play.

- A dead body on page 1 promises a mystery.
- A meet-cute on page 1 promises a romance.
- A system notification on page 1 promises LitRPG mechanics.
- A dragon on page 1 promises fantasy.

If you promise one genre and deliver another, the reader will put the book down even if the writing is excellent. They didn't come for what you're serving.

### Stakes Promise
The scale of conflict established early tells the reader what to expect.

- Personal stakes (one relationship, one choice) promise an intimate story.
- Community stakes (a town, a school, a company) promise a social story.
- Global/cosmic stakes (the world, the galaxy) promise an epic story.

You can ESCALATE stakes. Starting personal and going global works. You cannot DEFLATE. Starting with "the world is ending" and narrowing to "one person's feelings" feels like a letdown.

### Satisfaction Promise
Genre determines what "satisfying" means:

| Genre | Satisfaction Type | What Readers Expect |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Romance | Emotional | HEA or HFN. The couple must end up together. |
| Mystery | Intellectual | The puzzle is solved. The reader can trace the logic. |
| Thriller | Cathartic | The threat is neutralized. Justice (or vengeance) is served. |
| Horror | Confrontational | The protagonist faces the fear. Survival is optional, confrontation is not. |
| Fantasy | Transformational | The protagonist is changed. The world is changed. Something was achieved at cost. |
| Literary | Revelatory | The reader sees something about life they hadn't seen before. Resolution is optional. |
| LitRPG | Progression | The protagonist leveled up, grew stronger, overcame a challenge through earned power. |
| Comedy | Release | The tension resolves in a way that makes the reader laugh or smile. |

## Betraying the Promise vs. Subverting Expectations

### Betrayal (Bad)
Betrayal breaks the contract. The reader signed up for X and got Y.

- Promising a romance and killing the love interest permanently
- Promising a mystery and never revealing the solution
- Promising a hopeful story and ending in nihilism
- Promising a LitRPG and abandoning the system mechanics in Act 3

Betrayal makes the reader feel cheated. They trusted you, and you wasted their time.

### Subversion (Good)
Subversion fulfills the promise in an unexpected way. The reader gets what they signed up for, but not how they expected.

- Promising a romance and delivering the HEA, but the couple both have to change in ways they didn't expect
- Promising a mystery and revealing the solution, but the solution makes the detective question everything they believe
- Promising a hero's journey and having the hero succeed, but at a cost that redefines what success means
- Promising a thriller and neutralizing the threat, but the real threat was inside the protagonist's organization

Subversion makes the reader feel the story was smarter than they expected. They got what they came for AND something they didn't know they wanted.

### The Test
Ask: "Would a reader of this genre feel that the ending delivered on what the opening promised?"

- If YES: You fulfilled the promise (with or without subversion). Good.
- If NO: You betrayed it. Rewrite the ending or rewrite the opening. They must align.

## How Theme Connects to Promise

The promise statement contains the theme in miniature:

> "This is a story about [character] who [faces conflict] and discovers **[thematic truth]** at the cost of **[sacrifice]**."

The thematic truth IS the answer to the central question.
The sacrifice IS the cost of arriving at that answer.

If your promise statement and your central question don't align, one of them is wrong.

**Aligned:**
- Central question: "Can you hold onto who you are when power changes you?"
- Promise: "...discovers that identity is a choice you make every day, at the cost of the power that was changing them."

**Misaligned:**
- Central question: "Can you hold onto who you are when power changes you?"
- Promise: "...discovers that true friendship is the real treasure, at the cost of a magic sword."
(The promise is about friendship. The question is about identity and power. They're different stories.)

## Writing Toward the Promise

Once you have your promise statement, it becomes a decision-making tool:

1. **Scene selection**: Does this scene advance the promise? Keep it. Does it wander away from the promise? Cut it or find the connection.
2. **Character arc**: Does the protagonist's journey move toward discovering the thematic truth? If not, recalibrate.
3. **Ending design**: Does the ending deliver the thematic truth at the specified cost? If the ending doesn't echo the promise, something drifted.
4. **Pacing decisions**: The promise tells you what readers are waiting for. Don't make them wait too long for the thing you promised.
