# Anti-Flat Scene Rules

Scenes go flat when nothing meaningful happens. These rules prevent empty chapters.

---

## The Three Tests

Every scene must pass ALL THREE:

### 1. The Change Test
**Question**: What is different at the end of this scene than at the beginning?

Acceptable changes:
- Character knows something new
- Relationship has shifted
- Obstacle has appeared or been overcome
- Stakes have risen
- Decision has been made
- Belief has been challenged

**If nothing changes, the scene is probably flat or belongs inside another scene.**

### 2. The Agency Test
**Question**: What does the protagonist CAUSE to happen?

Acceptable agency:
- Makes a decision with consequences
- Takes an action that affects the situation
- Says something that changes a relationship
- Chooses between options (even bad ones)
- Refuses to act (and faces consequences)

**If the protagonist only reacts, witnesses, or feels, look for the choice their reaction produces or consider merging the scene.**

### 3. The Necessity Test
**Question**: What would be lost if this scene were cut?

Acceptable necessities:
- Plot information that can't be delivered elsewhere
- Character moment that defines who they are
- Relationship beat essential to later payoff
- Atmosphere/tone establishment (opening only)
- Setup for later reversal

**If the scene can be cut without loss, it is likely flat or redundant.**

## Common Flatness Patterns

### The Travel Chapter
Characters journey from A to B. They talk. They arrive.

**The Problem**: Movement isn't story.

**The Fix**:
- Something happens during travel that changes the situation
- Use travel for conflict, revelation, or relationship shift
- OR time-skip: "Three days later, they reached the city."

### The Processing Chapter
Big event just happened. Characters discuss their feelings about it.

**The Problem**: Reaction without action.

**The Fix**:
- Processing must lead to a decision
- Conversation must reveal conflict or new information
- Physical action anchors the emotional beat
- Character must DO something as a result of processing

### The Setup Chapter
This chapter exists only to position characters for the next one.

**The Problem**: No internal value.

**The Fix**:
- Tie the setup to a consequential choice, cost, discovery, or relationship shift
- Make an existing obstacle matter during the setup
- Merge with the chapter it is setting up
- Do not add an unrelated subplot or argument merely to manufacture motion

### The Information Chapter
Characters explain things to each other (or the reader).

**The Problem**: Exposition without drama.

**The Fix**:
- Information costs something to obtain
- Delivery creates conflict
- Recipient doesn't want to hear it
- Information changes the recipient's plans immediately

### The Repetition Chapter
Same emotional beat as the previous chapter.

**The Problem**: Reader feels stuck.

**The Fix**:
- Vary emotional register (fear → anger → resolve → grief → determination)
- If same emotion, escalate intensity
- Add contradiction (they think they're fine, but they're not)

## Agency Failures

### The Witness
Protagonist watches things happen.

**Fix**: Make them a participant. Their presence must matter.

### The Passenger
Protagonist is carried along by others' decisions.

**Fix**: Give them a choice, even if it's between bad options.

### The Feeler
Protagonist has emotions about events.

**Fix**: Emotions must drive action. Feel, then DO.

### The Explainer
Protagonist's role is to receive or deliver information.

**Fix**: Information must change what they do next, immediately.

### The Victim
Things happen TO the protagonist.

**Fix**: Even victims choose how to respond. Show the choice.

## The Momentum Check

Every scene should create forward pull. Check for:

### Hooks
- Unanswered question raised
- Threat established but not resolved
- Decision pending
- Anticipation for upcoming event
- Character in motion toward goal

### Anti-Hooks (avoid)
- Everything wrapped up neatly
- Character at rest with no pending action
- Question answered without new question raised
- Threat eliminated without new stakes
- Pause in action without tension

## Scene Energy Levels

Not every scene is a chase. But every scene needs SOME energy.

| Energy Level | Characteristics | When to Use |
|--------------|-----------------|-------------|
| **High** | Action, danger, revelation | Climax, turning points |
| **Medium** | Tension, conflict, discovery | Most scenes |
| **Low** | Reflection, connection, setup | Breathing room, aftermath |
| **Flat** | Nothing changes and no meaningful aftereffect develops | Merge, cut, or rebuild |

**Rule**: Never have two low-energy scenes in a row without a reason.

---

## The "So What?" Test

After writing a scene, ask: "So what?"

If you can't immediately answer with something that matters to the plot, characters, or reader experience, the scene is flat.

**Acceptable answers**:
- "So now she knows who betrayed her."
- "So now they trust each other."
- "So now the clock is ticking."
- "So now he has to choose."

**Unacceptable answers**:
- "So now the reader knows what the city looks like."
- "So now we see their daily routine."
- "So now we understand the magic system better."
- "So it shows they're friends."

Setting, routine, systems, and relationships are conveyed THROUGH scenes that matter, not in dedicated "explanation" scenes.
