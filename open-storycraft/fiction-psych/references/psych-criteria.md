# Psych Pass Criteria

## What a Good Interior Pass Does

A good psych pass makes the writing agent understand what it feels like to live inside this character for this chapter — not what happens to them, but what it costs them. The writing agent reads this alongside the scene summary. The scene summary tells them what to put on the page. The psych pass tells them what's happening underneath the page.

---

## The Core Problem You Are Solving

AI writing defaults to reporting from outside the character. The prose observes, notes, and states. The character "felt nervous." They "noticed the bottle." They "considered their options." This is the camera filming the room from across the street.

A psych pass moves the camera underwater. The character doesn't notice the bottle — the bottle is already inside their body before they register it consciously. Their shoulders drop a half-inch. They take the slow breath they don't know they're taking. The thought arrives not as narration but as a word: *of course.*

Your job is to map that interior layer so specifically that the writing agent cannot default to the outside view.

---

## Rules for Writing Each Section

### Start State

**Bad:** "Teo is tired and worried about money."

**Good:** "The exhaustion has gone past tired into mean. He has run the math eighty times today. Each time it comes out the same and each time it feels like the number is mocking him personally. There is a specific texture of self-disgust to running the math anyway — the way you keep checking a pocket for keys you know aren't there. He is a smart kid doing stupid kid math because the math is all he has."

The difference: the bad version names a state. The good version renders the texture of the state from inside the character's specific psychology — their intelligence, their situation, their self-image.

---

### Beat-by-Beat Interior Cost

Every external beat has an interior cost. The cost is specific to who this person is.

**The question to ask for each beat:** What does this beat mean to a person with this backstory, this want, this need, this flaw, this secret?

**Bad interior cost:** "He feels sad when he sees the photo."

**Good interior cost:** "The photo should not be a sad detail; it should be the most painful object in the apartment and Teo should not be able to look at it for more than a beat. The current draft notes it. The interior should flinch off it."

The difference: the bad version names an emotion. The good version names the specific WAY the emotion manifests in THIS character — including what the prose must do to render it (can't hold the gaze, flinches off it).

---

### Body Expression

Every interior state has a physical expression. Pull from the character's emotional vocabulary in the style guide.

**The question:** How does THIS character's body carry THIS emotion?

If the character's profile says fear makes their hands go still and flat: fear looks like hands going still and flat, not like a pounding heart or shallow breathing.

If the character expresses grief by doing the task in front of them: grief looks like picking up the fork and eating the rice, not like welling eyes.

The body expression is the writing agent's instruction for what to put on the page.

---

### What They Won't Say

The swallowed thought should sound like the character's internal register. Not literary. Not elegant. Blunt, in their voice.

**Bad:** "He felt a profound sense of alienation from his circumstances."

**Good:** "Stupid. Stupid." or "Of course. Of fucking course." or "I cannot keep doing this."

One word or one sentence. The thought that arrives fully formed and gets buried immediately. This is the character's real response before the performance of composure kicks in.

---

### Show-Don't-Tell Gaps

These must be specific beats from the chapter summary, not general advice.

**Bad:** "The prose should show more interiority throughout."

**Good:**

| Beat | What a reporting draft does | What it should do instead |
|------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| Seeing the ConEd bill on the table | Notes that Fernando "set it there deliberately" | A beat of jaw-lock. A swallow. The specific thought he won't say out loud: *don't do that. Don't do the thing where you can't ask me and you make me see it.* No words on the page. The body. |
| Counting $17 from the drawer | Counts the bills neutrally | The count feels humiliating. $17 in a grown man's drawer with expired coupons. The neutrality is the failure. The drawer should stick on the way back and the push past it carries the thing he is not letting out his mouth. |

The third column is always a specific alternative — not "show more emotion" but "do this specific thing with the body or the object or the silence."

---

## The Bottom Line Test

After you write the bottom line, ask: if the writing agent reads only this paragraph, will they understand what it feels like to be inside this character for this chapter?

If the answer is "they'll know what happens" — rewrite it. The bottom line is about what it costs, not what occurs.

**Bad bottom line:** "This chapter establishes Teo's financial situation and his temptation to call Machete."

**Good bottom line:** "This chapter is a man drowning quietly in a room. The prose must film it from underwater. Teo is not observing his life — he is trying to survive it in twenty-two-second increments, using the train count as a thing to do with his attention that is not picking up the phone. The flat hand at the end is the chapter's whole emotional argument: the body has decided. He has not. That gap is everything."
