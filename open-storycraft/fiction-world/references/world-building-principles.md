# World-Building Principles

Guiding philosophy for creating functional, story-serving worlds.

---

## Build Only What Matters

### Include
- Settings where scenes happen
- Rules that create conflict
- Culture that shapes character behavior
- Details that create atmosphere
- History that characters reference or act on

### Skip
- History no one mentions
- Places no one visits
- Rules that don't affect the plot
- Deep lore that never surfaces
- Systems explained to the reader but never used

### The Test
For every world element, ask: "Does this appear in a scene, affect a character decision, or create conflict?" If no, cut it or save it for later.

---

## World as Constraint

Every major world element should do at least one of these:

### Enable Conflict
The magic system allows duels but not healing. The social structure puts protagonist at a disadvantage. The geography isolates help.

### Limit Solutions
Characters can't just fly away because X. They can't call for help because Y. The obvious solution is blocked because Z.

### Force Difficult Choices
The rules mean protagonist must choose between A and B. Cultural taboos mean doing the right thing has social cost.

### Example
**Weak**: "Magic exists and can do many things."
**Strong**: "Magic exists but requires blood. Yours or someone else's. Every spell is a choice."

---

## Leave Room for Mystery

### Not Everything Explained
Some things exist without full explanation. Characters can wonder about the same things readers wonder about.

### Debated History
Characters disagree about what happened. Records were lost. Stories conflict. This creates space for discovery.

### Rules Not Fully Understood
Characters know magic works but not exactly why. They know the dangers but not the limits. This allows surprises.

### The Value
Mystery creates:
- Space for discovery (characters and readers)
- Sense that the world is larger than the story
- Flexibility for the plot
- Wonder

---

## Ground the Extraordinary

### Daily Life
Even in fantastical worlds, show how ordinary people live. What do they eat? How do they travel? What do they worry about?

### Mundane Details
The more extraordinary your world, the more important small realistic details become. They anchor the reader.

### Logical Consequences
If dragons exist, what does that mean for architecture? Insurance? Agriculture? Think through implications.

---

## World Reveals Character

### What Characters Notice
POV character's observations reveal their priorities, background, and biases. A soldier notices defensive positions. An artist notices light.

### What's Normal to Them
Characters don't explain what's normal to them. Use this for subtle world-building. They don't think about gravity; they don't explain magic if it's always existed.

### Contrast Points
When characters are surprised by something, readers learn both about the world and the character's expectations.

---

## Economy of World-Building

### Front-Load Sparingly
Don't explain everything at the start. Reveal world details as they become relevant.

### Integrate with Action
World-building lands better during scenes than in exposition. Show the rule being applied, not explained.

### Trust the Reader
You don't need to explain everything. Readers piece together worlds from context. Let them work a little.
