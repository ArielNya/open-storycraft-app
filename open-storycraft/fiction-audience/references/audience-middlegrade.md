# Audience: Middle Grade (Ages 10-14)

## Reader Profile
- **Age range**: 10-14 years old
- **Protagonist age norm**: 11-13
- **Reading level**: Upper elementary to middle school
- **Attention span**: Moderate. Can handle longer chapters and subplots, but still needs regular payoffs.

## Content Boundaries

### Violence
- **Allowed**: Combat, physical danger, injury, battle sequences, consequences of violence
- **Boundaries**: No graphic gore, no torture scenes, no detailed descriptions of suffering. Characters can be hurt and show pain, but the camera doesn't linger on wounds.
- **Rule**: Violence has weight and consequences, but is not fetishized. A sword cuts, a person falls, someone tends the wound. You don't describe the anatomy of the injury.
- **Death**: Can occur on-page for non-central characters. Mentor death is a genre staple. Peer death is handled with gravity but not graphic detail. No child death played for shock.

### Romance
- **Allowed**: Crushes, first-kiss territory, awkward romantic tension, blushing and stammering
- **Boundaries**: No making out, no sexual tension, no physical descriptions beyond hand-holding/first kiss. No "heat."
- **Rule**: Romance is a subplot, never the A-plot. It adds dimension to the protagonist but doesn't drive the story.

### Language
- **Allowed**: Mild language ("damn," "hell," "crap"). Characters can be crude in age-appropriate ways.
- **Boundaries**: No F-words, no sexual language, no slurs.
- **Rule**: The character can express real frustration. The language should match what an actual 12-year-old might say when their parents aren't listening.

### Darkness
- **Allowed**: Real stakes, genuine danger, themes of loss and grief, morally gray situations, flawed authority figures
- **Boundaries**: No sustained hopelessness, no nihilism, no graphic depictions of abuse (though its effects can be shown). No self-harm depicted as solution.
- **Rule**: The world is more complex than it seemed in childhood, but it is not irredeemable. Hope must exist, even if it requires work.

## Thematic Expectations

Middle grade readers are at the threshold of identity formation. These themes resonate deeply:

- **Identity Formation**: "Who am I?" is the central question of this age. The protagonist discovers qualities about themselves they didn't know existed.
- **Standing Up for What's Right**: The moral universe gets more complex. Sometimes the right thing is hard, unpopular, or costly. Do it anyway.
- **Navigating Social Hierarchy**: Cliques, popularity, exclusion, loyalty under pressure. The social world becomes a battleground.
- **First Real Friendships**: Deeper than "we play together." These friendships involve sacrifice, disagreement, forgiveness.
- **Questioning Authority**: Not yet full rebellion (that's YA), but noticing that adults are fallible. Teachers can be wrong. Parents don't have all the answers. Systems aren't always fair.
- **Competence & Mastery**: Discovering you're good at something. Training, practice, earning skill. The satisfaction of getting better.
- **Family Complexity**: Family is no longer just "safe background." Divorce, absent parents, sibling rivalry, chosen family vs. blood family.

## Vocabulary & Complexity Guidance

- **Sentence complexity**: Moderate. Can handle compound sentences, some subordinate clauses, varied paragraph lengths. Still avoid academic prose.
- **Vocabulary**: Richer than children's fiction. Can use challenging words in context. Scientific, historical, or fantastical terminology is fine when explained organically.
- **Reference pool**: School, friend groups, social media (age-appropriate), games, sports, music, YouTube/streaming culture, early independence (biking somewhere alone, first phone).
- **Internal monologue**: More present than children's fiction. The protagonist can reflect, worry, plan. But it should still be concrete: "She'd messed up. She knew it. The question was whether Maya knew it too." Not philosophical.
- **Abstraction tolerance**: Low-moderate. Can handle simple thematic questions ("Is it ever okay to lie to protect someone?") but needs them grounded in specific situations, not abstract debate.

## Structural Norms

- **Chapter length**: 2,000-3,000 words
- **Total word count**: 40,000-70,000 words
- **Chapter count**: 18-30 chapters
- **Pacing**: Moderate-fast. Subplots can breathe, but every chapter needs forward motion. Can sustain 2-3 chapters of rising tension before needing a release.
- **Series potential**: Very strong. Trilogies, 5-book series, open-ended series all work. Each book should have a satisfying conclusion while advancing the series arc.
- **Subplots**: Can handle 2-3 subplots alongside the main plot (friendship, family, school alongside the adventure/mystery/fantasy quest).

## Structural Rules

- **POV**: Single POV preferred. Dual POV possible but challenging. Multi-POV rarely works at this age.
- **Clear stakes**: The reader must always know what the protagonist stands to lose.
- **Adults are present**: They have roles, opinions, and authority, but they are not infallible and not the solution to the core problem.
- **The protagonist must have genuine agency**: Things don't just happen to them. They make choices, and those choices have consequences.
- **Endings**: Can be bittersweet but must contain genuine resolution. The protagonist should be changed by the experience.

## Successful Examples (Pattern Reference)

- Percy Jackson (later books): Escalating complexity, dual POV in sequels, Greek mythology framework
- The Hunger Games (Suzanne Collins classified it as MG in early development, though it's often shelved YA)
- Keeper of the Lost Cities: Fantasy world, school setting, friend group dynamics, slow-burn mystery
- Gregor the Overlander: Underground world, genuine danger, found family, sacrifice themes
- The One and Only Ivan: Animal POV, emotional depth, themes of captivity and freedom, gentle but real
