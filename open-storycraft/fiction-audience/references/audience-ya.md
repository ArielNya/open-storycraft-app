# Audience: Young Adult (Ages 14-18)

## Reader Profile
- **Age range**: 14-18 years old (actual readership skews 12-25)
- **Protagonist age norm**: 15-18
- **Reading level**: High school and above
- **Attention span**: Can handle complex narratives, multiple POVs, longer chapters, and slower pacing when earned

## Content Boundaries

### Violence
- **Allowed**: Combat, war, injury, death, consequences of violence depicted with weight
- **Boundaries**: Violence should serve the story, not exist for spectacle. Graphic violence is acceptable when it underscores stakes or character transformation, but gratuitous torture porn is not.
- **Rule**: If a character dies, it matters. If someone is hurt, there are consequences. Violence is not casual.
- **Death**: On-page for any character, including peers and loved ones. Can be graphic enough to convey the horror of violence without being exploitative.

### Romance
- **Allowed**: Full romantic arcs, kissing, physical attraction described, fade-to-black intimacy, sexual tension, first sexual experiences (depending on publisher/imprint)
- **Boundaries**: Explicit sex scenes are rare in traditional YA publishing. Fade to black or "morning after" is the norm. Self-published YA can push further.
- **Rule**: Romance can be the A-plot or B-plot. Physical attraction is acknowledged, not sanitized. Characters can want each other. But the focus is emotional, not erotic.

### Language
- **Allowed**: Realistic language including occasional F-words (1-3 per book is common in traditional publishing), crude humor, authentic teen speech
- **Boundaries**: Slurs should be handled with extreme care and only in service of themes about prejudice/identity. No gratuitous cruelty in language.
- **Rule**: Teens curse. Pretending they don't is condescending. But language should feel authentic, not edgy for the sake of it.

### Darkness
- **Allowed**: Depression, anxiety, grief, abuse (emotional and physical), addiction, eating disorders, suicidal ideation (handled responsibly), systemic injustice, moral complexity, no-win situations
- **Boundaries**: Dark content must be handled responsibly. Characters struggling with mental health should not be romanticized. Self-harm is depicted as a problem to address, not a solution.
- **Rule**: YA can go dark. Really dark. But it must go dark WITH PURPOSE. Darkness without hope is not YA; it's grimdark for adults. There must be a path forward, even if the protagonist doesn't take it cleanly.

## Thematic Expectations — MANDATORY

These are NON-NEGOTIABLE for YA. A YA novel that doesn't engage with at least 3-4 of these will feel hollow to its audience:

- **Fighting Authority**: The system is broken. Adults in power are wrong, corrupt, or blind. The protagonist must resist, subvert, or dismantle an unjust system. This is THE defining theme of YA.
- **Identity Crisis**: "Who am I?" becomes "Who do I CHOOSE to be?" The protagonist is not discovering their identity — they are forging it under pressure. This includes gender, sexuality, cultural identity, moral identity.
- **First Independence**: Making decisions without a safety net. Leaving home (literally or metaphorically). Standing alone for the first time and discovering whether you can.
- **Found Family**: When blood family fails you (or isn't enough), you build your own. The friend group, the squad, the crew. Loyalty tested and earned.
- **Betrayal by Trusted Adults**: A parent, mentor, teacher, or leader reveals they are not who the protagonist thought. This is not just "adults are dumb" — it's the shattering realization that the people who were supposed to protect you have their own agendas.
- **The System Is Broken**: Institutions (governments, schools, religions, corporations) that are supposed to serve people instead exploit them. The protagonist sees behind the curtain.
- **Choosing Who You Want to Be**: The climax of identity work. After everything they've been through, the protagonist makes a defining choice that says "this is who I am." This choice should cost something.
- **First Love / Heartbreak**: Not just romance — the intensity of feeling something for the first time. The vulnerability of opening yourself to another person. The devastation when it doesn't work.

## Vocabulary & Complexity Guidance

- **Sentence complexity**: Unrestricted. YA prose can be as sophisticated as adult prose. What changes is the emotional register, not the reading level.
- **Vocabulary**: No restrictions. YA readers have strong vocabularies. Use the right word, not the simple word.
- **Reference pool**: Social media, music, movies/TV, internet culture, memes, school dynamics, part-time jobs, driving, parties, college anxiety, family financial stress.
- **Internal monologue**: Central to YA. The protagonist's inner voice is often the strongest element. Can be extended, raw, contradictory, and emotionally intense. This is where voice lives.
- **Abstraction tolerance**: Moderate-high. Can engage with philosophical and moral questions, but they must connect to the protagonist's lived experience, not exist in abstract.

## Structural Norms

- **Chapter length**: 2,500-4,000 words
- **Total word count**: 60,000-90,000 words
- **Chapter count**: 20-35 chapters
- **Pacing**: Variable. Can sustain slower introspective sections when voice is strong. Action sequences should be tight. The overall arc should accelerate.
- **Series potential**: Trilogies are the gold standard. Duologies work for romance-forward YA. Standalones are increasingly popular for contemporary YA. Open-ended series (5+) are less common.
- **Multiple POV**: Common. Dual POV is popular (especially in romance). Triple POV works. Beyond that, be careful.

## Structural Rules

- **The protagonist MUST have agency**: YA readers will not tolerate a passive protagonist. Things cannot just happen to them. They must make choices, even bad ones.
- **Adults cannot solve the problem**: Adults can help, advise, sacrifice, even die. But the core conflict must be resolved by the teen protagonist's choices and actions.
- **The ending must deliver transformation**: The protagonist at the end is fundamentally different from the protagonist at the start. They have earned something through suffering, choice, or sacrifice.
- **Stakes must be personal AND structural**: It's not enough that the world is at risk. The protagonist must also stand to lose something personal (a relationship, their identity, their home, their innocence).
- **Voice is everything**: A YA novel with a weak voice is dead on arrival. The protagonist's voice must be distinctive, authentic, and compelling enough to carry the reader through slower sections.

## Successful Examples (Pattern Reference)

- The Hunger Games: Fighting authority, survival, identity under pressure, found family, the system is broken
- Six of Crows: Found family, heist structure, diverse cast, romance as subplot, morally gray protagonist
- The Hate U Give: Systemic injustice, identity (code-switching), fighting authority, family complexity, finding your voice
- Children of Blood and Bone: Fighting authority, magical system, cultural identity, chosen one subverted, first love
- A Court of Thorns and Roses (Book 1): Often shelved YA though later books go NA. Romance-forward, world-crossing, identity transformation
