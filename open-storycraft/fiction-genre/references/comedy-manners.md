# Comedy of Manners

**Definition**: Fiction that derives its humor from the social conventions, class dynamics, and behavioral codes of a specific community, exposing the gap between what people say and what they mean.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Social Performance**: Characters are constantly performing for their social circle, and the comedy comes from the distance between their public behavior and private thoughts.
- **Wit as Weapon**: Intelligence and verbal dexterity determine social standing. The cleverest speakers win, and the dullest are the butts of jokes.
- **Class Awareness**: The characters are acutely conscious of social hierarchy, whether aristocratic, professional, academic, or suburban. Status is always being negotiated.
- **Hypocrisy Exposed**: Characters who preach one thing and practice another are systematically revealed and punctured by the narrative.

## Optional Tropes
- **The Outsider Who Sees Clearly**: A character from outside the social group who perceives the absurdity that insiders cannot.
- **Marriage as Transaction**: Romantic relationships viewed through the lens of social advantage, financial security, or familial obligation.
- **Gossip as Plot Engine**: Information travels through whisper networks, and reputation can be made or destroyed by rumor.
- **The Set Piece Social Event**: A dinner party, ball, wedding, or gathering where all social tensions converge.
- **Letters, Texts, or Communications**: Private correspondence that reveals true feelings, often contrasting sharply with public behavior.
- **The Foolish Authority Figure**: A patriarch, matriarch, or social leader whose pronouncements are treated as gospel but are clearly ridiculous.
- **Ironic Narrator**: A narrative voice that comments dryly on the characters' pretensions while appearing to take them seriously.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: You will see through the polite facades of a specific social world and laugh at the machinery underneath.
- **Resolution**: Social order is either restored with new understanding, or the protagonist escapes its constraints. Ironic endings are common.
- **Pacing**: Measured and dialogue-heavy. The pleasure is in conversation and observation, not action.
- **Tone**: Dry, sophisticated, and knowing. The humor is cerebral rather than physical.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The intellectual pleasure of social observation, the delight of watching pretension punctured, and the recognition of similar dynamics in the reader's own life.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establishes the social world, its rules, and the narrator's ironic stance within the first chapter. No rush.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-6,000 words. Room for scenes of social interaction to develop and for wit to accumulate.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Rare. Chapters end on revelations, reversals of social fortune, or perfectly placed bon mots.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act or episodic. The structure often mirrors the social calendar (a season, a visit, a courtship).

## Comp Titles
1. **Pride and Prejudice** by Jane Austen — The foundational comedy of manners: marriage, class, and the comedy of misreading character within rigid social codes.
2. **The Remains of the Day** by Kazuo Ishiguro — A butler's devotion to propriety as a comedy of repression and missed human connection.
3. **The Age of Innocence** by Edith Wharton — Gilded Age New York society as a beautiful prison, where manners enforce conformity with devastating effectiveness.
4. **Eligible** by Curtis Sittenfeld — A modern retelling of Austen set in Cincinnati, proving the comedy of manners translates perfectly to contemporary social dynamics.
5. **The House of Mirth** by Edith Wharton — Society's rules as a trap that destroys those who cannot play the game well enough, comedy shading into tragedy.

## Themes That Work
- **Appearance vs. Reality**: The entire subgenre is built on this tension. What people present to the world versus who they actually are is an inexhaustible comedy engine.
- **The Cost of Conformity**: Social rules exist to maintain order, but they also crush individuality. The characters who cannot perform correctly suffer, and the comedy is in watching them try.
- **Language as Power**: In a world where direct speech is forbidden, those who master indirection, implication, and wit hold all the cards.
- **The Marriage Market**: Romantic partnerships as economic and social transactions, with love as an inconvenient variable that disrupts rational calculation.
- **Self-Knowledge**: The journey from social performance to genuine self-understanding, often arrived at through humiliation.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Meanness Mistaken for Wit**: Characters who are simply cruel to others are not funny. Wit requires intelligence and specificity. A clever observation about someone's pretensions is comedy; a blunt insult is not.
- **Static Social World**: If nothing in the social order is ever threatened or changed, the comedy has no stakes. Someone must risk something, even if the risk is purely social.
- **Inaccessible Social Code**: If the reader cannot understand the rules being satirized, they cannot appreciate the comedy. The social world must be clearly established before it can be effectively mocked.
