# Historical Romance

**Definition**: Romance set in a historically recognizable period (Regency, Victorian, medieval, etc.) where the era's social constraints, customs, and power structures shape the courtship and conflict.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **HEA/HFN**: The couple must achieve a happily ever after or happy for now ending.
- **Society as Obstacle**: Class, propriety, or period-specific social rules create barriers the couple must overcome.
- **Historical Grounding**: Setting details (clothing, speech patterns, politics) must feel authentic even when selectively deployed.
- **Forbidden or Unconventional Match**: The romance crosses a line the era would not permit easily (rank, wealth, scandal).

## Optional Tropes
- **Marriage of Convenience**: A practical union that becomes real love.
- **Wallflower Transformation**: A socially overlooked character proves magnetic to the love interest.
- **Rake Reformed**: A notorious libertine falls genuinely for one person.
- **Secret Identity**: One party conceals their true rank, name, or past.
- **Bluestocking Heroine**: An intellectual woman who defies her era's expectations.
- **Enemies to Lovers**: Rival families, political opponents, or feuding neighbors.
- **Compromised Reputation**: A scandal forces proximity or marriage.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: Escape into a richly drawn past where love conquers rigid social rules.
- **Resolution**: The couple unites despite every societal barrier, often with public validation (wedding, acceptance by family/ton).
- **Pacing**: Slow to moderate buildup with escalating tension through stolen moments and social maneuvering.
- **Tone**: Elegant, witty, sometimes lush. Regency skews lighter; medieval and Gothic skew darker.
- **Satisfaction Source**: Watching two people outwit an entire social system to be together.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Moderate. Establish the world and the social stakes within the first two chapters, but the hook is often a charged first meeting.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional. Chapters tend to end on social tension or emotional revelation rather than action cliffhangers.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act. Act 1 introduces the match and the social barrier. Act 2 deepens the relationship through forced proximity and escalating stakes. Act 3 resolves with a grand gesture or public declaration.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Duke and I** by Julia Quinn — Regency wit, marriage-of-convenience spark, society as playground and cage.
2. **Outlander** by Diana Gabaldon — Epic historical sweep blending adventure, war, and a passionate central romance.
3. **The Duchess Deal** by Tessa Dare — Humor-forward Regency with a scarred duke and a seamstress, showcasing class-crossing romance.
4. **A Knight in Shining Armor** by Jude Deveraux — Medieval time-travel romance that leans into chivalric ideals and fish-out-of-water tension.
5. **Devil in Winter** by Lisa Kleypas — Victorian rake-and-wallflower pairing with emotional depth and a slow-burn power shift.

## Themes That Work
- **Freedom vs. Duty**: Characters torn between personal desire and family/social obligation.
- **Class and Worth**: Proving that character matters more than birth or title.
- **Hidden Strength**: A protagonist underestimated by their era reveals quiet power.
- **Legacy and Change**: Love as the catalyst for challenging outdated systems.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Modern Mindset in Period Dress**: Characters who think, speak, and act with 21st-century sensibilities destroy immersion. The protagonists can be progressive for their era, but they must still exist within it.
- **Wallpaper Historicals**: Using a period setting as decoration without letting it shape the conflict. If the story works identically in a modern setting, the history is cosmetic.
- **Info-Dumping Research**: Paragraphs of historical detail that stall the romance. Weave setting through action and dialogue, not lectures.
