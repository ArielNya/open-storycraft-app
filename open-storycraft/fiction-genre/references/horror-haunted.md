# Haunted House

**Definition**: Horror where the primary antagonist is a location itself, a house, building, or place that is fundamentally wrong, and exerts malevolent influence on anyone who enters.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **The Location as Antagonist**: The house, hotel, asylum, or other structure is not merely a setting but an active force with agency, history, and intent.
- **Wrongness of Place**: Something about the location is perceptibly off, whether through spatial anomalies, temperature, sound, or an ineffable sense of dread that the characters cannot rationalize away.
- **Escalating Manifestations**: The hauntings begin subtly (cold spots, strange sounds, objects moved) and intensify toward direct confrontation.
- **Resistance to Leaving**: Characters are prevented from leaving, whether by physical barriers, psychological compulsion, practical necessity, or the house's direct intervention.
- **History of Trauma**: The location's malevolence is rooted in past events (deaths, abuse, rituals, construction on wrong ground) that created or attracted the haunting.

## Optional Tropes
- **Spatial Distortion**: Rooms that should not exist, hallways that change length, architecture that defies the building's exterior dimensions.
- **The Sensitive Character**: One person who perceives the haunting more strongly than others, often dismissed by the group.
- **Previous Victims**: Evidence of others who lived in or visited the location and suffered similar fates.
- **The Skeptic**: A character who refuses to accept the supernatural explanation, providing tension within the group.
- **Personalized Hauntings**: The house targets each character individually, exploiting their specific fears, guilt, or desires.
- **Research and Discovery**: Characters uncover the location's history through documents, records, or local accounts.
- **The Caretaker or Neighbor**: A local who knows the location's reputation and either warns or misleads the protagonists.
- **Possession or Absorption**: The house does not just haunt its inhabitants but attempts to claim, consume, or become them.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: A slow-building, atmospheric experience where the setting itself becomes the most terrifying character in the story.
- **Resolution**: Escape (with knowledge of the true nature of the place), destruction of the location, or the tragic failure to leave. The house may win.
- **Pacing**: Slow build with a long atmospheric first half that accelerates sharply in the second.
- **Tone**: Claustrophobic, oppressive, increasingly unhinged as the location's influence grows.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The immersive dread of being trapped somewhere wrong, the creativity of the hauntings, and the revelation of what made the place this way.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Slow. The arrival, the first impressions, the settling in. The wrongness should be felt before it is understood.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words. Enough space for atmospheric description and the slow ratcheting of tension.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Escalating. Early chapters end on unease; later chapters end on terror.
- **Act Structure**: Invasion model. Act 1: arrival, subtle wrongness, characters commit to staying. Act 2: manifestations escalate, the house's history emerges, attempts to leave fail. Act 3: full confrontation with the house's true nature, escape or consumption.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Haunting of Hill House** by Shirley Jackson — The foundational modern haunted house novel, where the house's influence on Eleanor is inseparable from her psychological fragility.
2. **House of Leaves** by Mark Z. Danielewski — A house that is larger inside than outside, told through nested unreliable narratives that mirror the spatial impossibility.
3. **The Shining** by Stephen King — The Overlook Hotel as a malevolent entity that exploits Jack Torrance's alcoholism and rage to claim his family.
4. **Kill Creek** by Scott Thomas — Four horror authors spend a night in a notorious haunted house, and something follows each of them home.
5. **The Elementals** by Michael McDowell — Three Victorian houses on the Gulf Coast, one of which is slowly being consumed by sand and something far worse.

## Themes That Work
- **Home as Prison**: The subversion of domestic safety, the place that should protect you becoming the thing that threatens you.
- **The Weight of History**: Places absorb the trauma that occurs within them, and that trauma is not passive.
- **Belonging and Consumption**: The desire to belong somewhere twisted into the horror of being claimed by somewhere.
- **Perception and Space**: The unsettling idea that the physical world is not fixed or reliable, that walls and rooms can lie.
- **Complicity**: Characters who stay too long, who are seduced by the house, who become part of the problem.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Jump scares without architecture**: Random scary events (doors slamming, figures appearing) without a coherent logic to the haunting produces a funhouse, not horror. The manifestations need to build, connect, and reveal.
- **Interchangeable characters**: If the people in the house are generic, the hauntings cannot be personalized, and the horror becomes impersonal. Each character needs a specific vulnerability the house can exploit.
- **Explaining too much**: The house's history should illuminate its nature, not fully explain it. Complete rational explanation (it was built on a burial ground, so ghosts) deflates the dread. Leave room for the unexplainable.
