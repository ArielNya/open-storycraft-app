# Whodunit

**Definition**: A mystery structured around a central crime (usually murder) where the reader is presented with clues, suspects, and red herrings, racing to identify the culprit before the detective reveals the answer.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **The Crime**: A significant crime (typically murder) that demands investigation and carries real stakes.
- **The Detective Figure**: A central investigator, professional or amateur, whose reasoning the reader follows.
- **Suspects and Motives**: Multiple plausible suspects, each with means, motive, and opportunity.
- **Fair Play Clues**: All evidence needed to solve the mystery is presented to the reader before the reveal.
- **The Reveal**: A climactic scene where the detective explains who did it and how, resolving all loose threads.

## Optional Tropes
- **Red Herrings**: Deliberately misleading clues planted to divert suspicion from the true culprit.
- **The Closed Circle**: A limited setting (island, mansion, train) that restricts the suspect pool.
- **The Watson Figure**: A companion character whose questions let the detective explain their reasoning.
- **The Least Likely Suspect**: The killer turns out to be someone the reader dismissed early on.
- **Multiple Murders**: Additional killings that raise stakes and narrow the suspect list.
- **The Ticking Clock**: An external deadline that pressures the investigation forward.
- **The False Solution**: A plausible but incorrect theory presented before the true answer.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: An intellectually honest puzzle that rewards attentive reading.
- **Resolution**: The killer is definitively identified and the method is fully explained; justice is typically served.
- **Pacing**: Escalating, with each new clue or interview building toward the reveal.
- **Tone**: Ranges from light and cerebral to dark and menacing, depending on subtype.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The "aha" moment when the solution clicks, whether the reader solved it first or not.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: The crime occurs within the first two chapters, often in chapter one.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,500-5,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Most chapters end on a new clue, revelation, or shift in suspicion.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act (crime/investigation/reveal) or four-act (crime/false leads/breakthrough/reveal).

## Comp Titles
1. **The 7 1/2 Deaths of Evelyn Hardcastle** by Stuart Turton — A classic whodunit structure elevated by a time-loop concept that lets the reader re-examine suspects from multiple angles.
2. **And Then There Were None** by Agatha Christie — The gold standard closed-circle whodunit where every character is both suspect and potential victim.
3. **The Maid** by Nita Prose — A modern whodunit with a distinctive protagonist whose unusual perspective shapes the investigation.
4. **In the Woods** by Tana French — A literary take on the whodunit that proves the form can carry deep character work.
5. **The Thursday Murder Club** by Richard Osman — A contemporary whodunit that balances humor with a genuinely clever puzzle.

## Themes That Work
- **Surfaces vs. truth**: Everyone presents a face to the world; the detective peels back the masks.
- **Order restored**: The crime disrupts the social fabric, and the solution stitches it back together.
- **The cost of secrets**: Hidden relationships, buried pasts, and unspoken grudges fuel the mystery.
- **Justice and its limits**: Whether legal punishment truly equals justice for the victim.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Unfair puzzle**: Withholding critical evidence from the reader until the reveal feels like cheating and breaks the implicit contract of the form.
- **Interchangeable suspects**: If every suspect blurs together, the reader cannot engage with the puzzle; each suspect needs a distinct personality and clear motive.
- **Anticlimactic reveal**: A solution that relies on coincidence, unknown information, or a character introduced late undermines everything the reader invested in solving.
