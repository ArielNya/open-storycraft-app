# Folk Horror

**Definition**: Horror rooted in rural landscapes, ancient traditions, and the collision between outsiders and insular communities whose customs conceal something dark, primal, and older than modernity.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Rural or Remote Setting**: The story takes place in the countryside, a village, an island, or a landscape where nature dominates and civilization feels thin.
- **Old Traditions**: The community practices rituals, festivals, or customs with roots in pagan, pre-Christian, or otherwise ancient belief systems.
- **The Outsider**: A character from the modern world who enters the community and gradually discovers what lies beneath its surface.
- **The Landscape as Force**: The natural environment is not backdrop but participant, embodying the ancient power that the community serves or fears.
- **Creeping Realization**: The outsider slowly understands that the community's quaint customs are not quaint at all, and by the time they fully understand, it may be too late.

## Optional Tropes
- **The Friendly Welcome**: The community initially appears hospitable, even charming, masking its true nature behind warmth and tradition.
- **Sacrifice**: The rituals demand something from the outsider, whether their participation, their body, or their life.
- **The Wicker Man Effect**: The outsider is not a random victim but was chosen or lured deliberately.
- **Seasonal or Calendar-Driven Plot**: The horror builds toward a specific date, festival, or seasonal event.
- **Corrupted Innocence**: Children, fertility, or harvest imagery twisted into something sinister.
- **Local Authority Complicity**: Police, clergy, or civic leaders are part of the conspiracy.
- **Archaeological or Historical Discovery**: The outsider uncovers physical evidence (standing stones, burial sites, carvings) of what the community worships.
- **Nature Reclaiming**: The boundary between the human and natural worlds dissolves, with the natural world winning.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: A sun-drenched, pastoral surface hiding something ancient and terrible underneath, with a slow-building sense that the protagonist is walking into a trap.
- **Resolution**: The outsider is consumed by the community's purpose (tragic), escapes with devastating knowledge (bittersweet), or becomes a willing convert (deeply unsettling). Clean escapes are rare.
- **Pacing**: Slow and deliberate, mirroring the rhythms of rural life. The horror creeps rather than jumps.
- **Tone**: Pastoral turned sinister, daylight horror, the uncanny in the familiar.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The slow-building dread of watching someone walk willingly into a trap they cannot see, and the reveal of what the community has been doing all along.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Slow. The journey to the location, the first impressions, the deceptive charm. The reader should feel the same initial comfort as the outsider.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words. The deliberate pace requires space for atmosphere and the accumulation of wrong details.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional. Tension builds through accumulating wrongness rather than dramatic revelations.
- **Act Structure**: Seduction model. Act 1: arrival, welcome, the outsider settles in. Act 2: cracks appear, the outsider investigates, each discovery raises stakes. Act 3: the true purpose is revealed, the outsider must act.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Wicker Man** (novelization by Robin Hardy and Anthony Shaffer) — The definitive folk horror story: a policeman investigates a missing girl on a Scottish island and discovers a pagan community building toward May Day.
2. **The Ritual** by Adam Nevill — Four friends hiking in Scandinavian wilderness encounter something ancient in the forest and a community that worships it.
3. **Harvest Home** by Thomas Tryon — A family moves to an idyllic New England village where the harvest traditions hide a horrifying secret.
4. **The Hunger** by Alma Katsu — The Donner Party's historical journey reimagined with folk horror elements, where the land and its spirits are as dangerous as starvation.
5. **Cunning Folk** by Adam Nevill — A family moves to a rural property and discovers their elderly neighbors practice something far older and darker than neighborly concern.

## Themes That Work
- **Modernity vs. the Ancient**: The hubris of assuming that progress has made old ways irrelevant or powerless.
- **Community and Conformity**: The horror of collective belief and the violence that communities commit together that individuals would not commit alone.
- **The Land Remembers**: Places hold power, memory, and demands that predate and outlast human civilization.
- **Outsider Perspective**: The vulnerability of being the only person who sees the truth in a place where everyone else is complicit.
- **The Seduction of Belonging**: The temptation to join, to be accepted, even when acceptance requires monstrous compromise.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Generic "creepy village" without specificity**: The community's traditions must feel specific, historically grounded, and internally coherent. Vague "pagan stuff" is not folk horror; it is set dressing.
- **The outsider as tourist**: If the protagonist treats the community as a curiosity to observe rather than engaging with it, the reader remains at the same safe distance. The outsider must be drawn in, invested, and compromised.
- **Revealing the horror too early**: Folk horror depends on the slow accumulation of wrongness. If the community's secret is exposed in act one, the subgenre's signature tension (the trap closing around someone who does not yet see it) is lost.
