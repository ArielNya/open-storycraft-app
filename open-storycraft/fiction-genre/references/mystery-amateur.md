# Amateur Sleuth

**Definition**: A mystery featuring a non-professional investigator whose specific occupation, hobby, or community role gives them unique access and expertise to solve crimes that baffle or elude the authorities.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Civilian Protagonist**: The investigator has no law enforcement training or authority; their day job is something else entirely.
- **Occupation as Investigative Tool**: The sleuth's profession or expertise provides specialized knowledge, access to suspects, or a unique angle on the crime.
- **Personal Stake**: The sleuth has a credible reason to investigate beyond curiosity: a friend is accused, their livelihood is threatened, or the crime happened in their domain.
- **Tension with Authorities**: Police are skeptical, obstructive, or incompetent, forcing the amateur to work around official channels.
- **Resourcefulness Over Resources**: Without badges, warrants, or record systems, the sleuth relies on social skills, specialized knowledge, and dogged persistence.

## Optional Tropes
- **Series-Friendly Structure**: Each book features a new case while the sleuth's personal life and relationships evolve across volumes.
- **Occupation-Themed Clues**: The mystery's solution connects to the sleuth's area of expertise (a chef notices a poisoning detail, a librarian recognizes a forged document).
- **Community Network**: The sleuth's social connections provide information, alibis, and suspects that police cannot access.
- **The Supportive Friend**: A confidant who helps brainstorm theories and sometimes gets into trouble.
- **Romantic Interest in Law Enforcement**: A detective or officer who alternates between being annoyed and impressed by the amateur's involvement.
- **The Sleuth in Danger**: Investigating without backup means the amateur eventually attracts the killer's attention.
- **Hobby or Setting Details**: Rich description of the sleuth's world (antique shop, restaurant kitchen, university campus) that immerses readers in a specific milieu.
- **Reluctant Investigator**: The sleuth does not want to get involved but circumstances leave them no choice.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: A relatable protagonist using wit and specialized knowledge to solve a puzzle that professionals cannot crack.
- **Resolution**: The amateur identifies the killer, usually through a combination of expertise, social intelligence, and courage; authorities then make the arrest.
- **Pacing**: Moderate, balancing investigation with the sleuth's daily life, relationships, and occupation scenes.
- **Tone**: Ranges from light and witty to suspenseful, depending on how much danger the amateur faces.
- **Satisfaction Source**: Watching an ordinary person outthink a killer using the skills they already have.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establishes the sleuth's world and occupation before the crime disrupts it, usually within the first three chapters.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,500-4,500 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional; chapters alternate between investigative momentum and character or occupation scenes.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act with the midpoint typically being when the sleuth commits fully to investigating despite warnings to stop.

## Comp Titles
1. **A is for Alibi** by Sue Grafton — Kinsey Millhone is technically a PI, but her independent, resourceful approach defined the modern amateur sleuth template.
2. **The No. 1 Ladies' Detective Agency** by Alexander McCall Smith — Precious Ramotswe uses common sense, social intelligence, and deep knowledge of her community to solve cases.
3. **One for the Money** by Janet Evanovich — Stephanie Plum's bounty hunting career launches from amateur desperation, with humor and occupation-driven investigations.
4. **The Sweetness at the Bottom of the Pie** by Alan Bradley — Eleven-year-old Flavia de Luce uses her chemistry expertise to investigate a murder in her village.
5. **An Unkindness of Ravens** by Ruth Rendell — Inspector Wexford's cases often feature civilians whose expertise proves essential to solving the crime.

## Themes That Work
- **Ordinary people, extraordinary situations**: Anyone can be called to act when injustice lands in their world.
- **Knowledge is power**: Specialized expertise levels the playing field between civilian and criminal.
- **Community matters**: The sleuth's web of relationships is both the source of the mystery and the means of solving it.
- **Identity through action**: Investigating the crime reveals who the sleuth truly is, beyond their occupation.

## Common Pitfalls
- **No credible reason to investigate**: If the amateur has no personal stake and no unique advantage, the reader wonders why they do not just call the police.
- **Occupation as wallpaper**: If the sleuth's job is mentioned but never meaningfully shapes the investigation, the occupation-themed promise is broken.
- **Superhuman amateur**: A civilian who outperforms trained detectives at every turn without consequence strains credulity; the amateur should struggle, make mistakes, and face real danger.
