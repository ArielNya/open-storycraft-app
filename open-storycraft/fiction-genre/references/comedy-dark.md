# Dark Comedy

**Definition**: Fiction that extracts humor from grim, taboo, or morally uncomfortable subject matter, using laughter as a lens for examining things too painful or absurd to face straight on.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Humor From Darkness**: The comedy is inseparable from its dark subject matter. Death, failure, cruelty, or suffering are the raw materials, not obstacles to the humor.
- **Tonal Tightrope**: The story balances genuine emotional weight with genuine laughs, never fully committing to either pure comedy or pure tragedy.
- **Morally Compromised Characters**: The cast includes people who are selfish, petty, deluded, or actively terrible, and the reader is invited to find them funny rather than (or in addition to) condemning them.
- **Gallows Humor**: Characters facing dire circumstances respond with jokes, understatement, or inappropriate levity as a coping mechanism.

## Optional Tropes
- **The Spiral**: A character's situation deteriorates steadily, and each new disaster is simultaneously worse and funnier than the last.
- **Ironic Justice**: Characters get exactly what they deserve in the most poetically awful way possible.
- **Social Satire**: The darkness targets specific institutions, classes, or cultural norms, revealing their cruelty through humor.
- **The Oblivious Protagonist**: A character who does not realize how terrible their situation (or they themselves) are, creating comedy through the gap between self-perception and reality.
- **Uncomfortable Sympathy**: The reader finds themselves rooting for or laughing with someone they probably should not.
- **Violence as Punchline**: Physical harm rendered in a tone that makes it comedic rather than disturbing, though the line is deliberately blurred.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: You will laugh at things you are not supposed to laugh at, and you will think about why.
- **Resolution**: Often ironic, tragic, or ambiguous. Happy endings are possible but must be earned through the darkness, not despite it.
- **Pacing**: Variable. Can be frenetic or slow-building, depending on whether the comedy is slapstick-dark or dry-dark.
- **Tone**: Uncomfortable. The reader should frequently be unsure whether to laugh or wince, ideally doing both.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The catharsis of laughing at the worst parts of life, combined with the intellectual pleasure of dark irony.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establish the dark comedic tone immediately. The reader needs to know what kind of book this is within the first scene.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words. Enough room for setups and payoffs that require context.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional. Chapters more often end on an ironic twist or a joke that recontextualizes everything.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act works well, with the second act as a downward spiral and the third as either redemption or spectacular collapse.

## Comp Titles
1. **A Confederacy of Dunces** by John Kennedy Toole — A deluded, insufferable protagonist rampages through New Orleans in a comic masterpiece of human failure.
2. **Slaughterhouse-Five** by Kurt Vonnegut — War, death, and time travel rendered in a tone that is simultaneously heartbroken and hilarious.
3. **My Year of Rest and Relaxation** by Ottessa Moshfegh — A privileged woman's attempt to sleep through life, darkly funny in its portrait of depression and disconnection.
4. **Lamb** by Christopher Moore — The untold story of Jesus's childhood friend, mining sacred material for irreverent comedy without cruelty.
5. **The Sellout** by Paul Beatty — A Black farmer in Los Angeles reinstates segregation and slavery in his neighborhood, a savagely funny satire of American race relations.

## Themes That Work
- **Mortality and Denial**: Death is the ultimate dark comedy subject. How people avoid thinking about it, how they respond when forced to, and how they carry on afterward.
- **Complicity**: Everyone participates in systems they know are wrong. Dark comedy makes the audience complicit in the laughter, mirroring how we are all complicit in the darkness of everyday life.
- **The Banality of Cruelty**: The funniest dark comedy often targets not dramatic evil but the small, petty, everyday cruelties people inflict on each other.
- **Coping Mechanisms**: Humor itself as a survival strategy, and the question of whether laughing at pain helps process it or helps avoid it.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Edgy Without Purpose**: Shocking content that exists only to shock is not dark comedy; it is provocation. The darkness must serve a comedic or thematic purpose beyond making the reader uncomfortable.
- **Punching Down**: Dark comedy about the powerful failing is satire. Dark comedy about the powerless suffering is cruelty. The target of the humor matters enormously.
- **Losing the Funny**: If the darkness overwhelms the comedy, you have written a tragedy with occasional jokes. The humor must remain a load-bearing element throughout.
