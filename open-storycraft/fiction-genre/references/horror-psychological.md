# Psychological Horror

**Definition**: Horror that originates from the mind itself, where the protagonist's perception, memory, or sanity becomes unreliable, and the reader cannot trust what is real.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Unreliable Perception**: The protagonist (and reader) cannot be certain what is actually happening versus what is imagined, hallucinated, or manufactured.
- **Internal Threat**: The horror comes from within the character's psyche or from manipulation by others, not from an external monster.
- **Paranoia and Doubt**: The protagonist questions their own sanity, memories, or the motives of those around them.
- **Reality Distortion**: The line between what is real and what is constructed blurs progressively throughout the narrative.
- **Psychological Vulnerability**: The protagonist has a pre-existing wound (trauma, grief, guilt, mental illness) that the horror exploits.

## Optional Tropes
- **Gaslighting**: Another character deliberately undermines the protagonist's grip on reality.
- **Isolation**: Physical or social isolation that removes the protagonist's ability to reality-check their experiences.
- **Doppelgangers or Doubles**: Encounters with versions of the self that challenge identity.
- **Repressed Memory**: Buried trauma that surfaces in distorted, terrifying forms.
- **Obsession**: A fixation that consumes the protagonist and warps their behavior and perception.
- **Ambiguous Ending**: The reader is left uncertain whether the horror was real, imagined, or both.
- **Confined Setting**: A single location (house, hospital, institution) that becomes a pressure cooker.
- **Unreliable Narrator**: The protagonist's account of events contradicts evidence or other characters' perspectives.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: The ground beneath the reader's feet will shift. Nothing can be taken at face value.
- **Resolution**: Revelation of truth (or deliberate refusal to confirm what was real), often with the protagonist fundamentally changed or destroyed by the experience.
- **Pacing**: Slow, deliberate escalation with disorienting shifts that accelerate toward the end.
- **Tone**: Unsettling, claustrophobic, intellectually disturbing.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The puzzle of reality, the dread of not knowing what to trust, and the devastating moment when the truth (or its absence) clicks.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Slow and grounded. The reader needs to trust the protagonist's perspective before that trust is systematically undermined.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words. Enough space for the disorientation to work without rushing the effect.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional, but reality-questioning rather than action-based. The cliffhanger is "wait, was that real?" not "will they survive?"
- **Act Structure**: Erosion model. Act 1: establish the protagonist's reality and vulnerability. Act 2: reality erodes through escalating distortions and contradictions. Act 3: collapse or revelation.

## Comp Titles
1. **House of Leaves** by Mark Z. Danielewski — A labyrinthine narrative where the structure of the book itself mirrors the protagonist's disintegrating reality.
2. **Gone Girl** by Gillian Flynn — Masterful perspective manipulation where neither narrator can be trusted and the reader's sympathies are deliberately weaponized.
3. **The Haunting of Hill House** by Shirley Jackson — A psychologically fragile protagonist in a house that may or may not be supernatural, where the true horror is Eleanor's mind.
4. **Sharp Objects** by Gillian Flynn — A journalist's return home unravels repressed trauma and reveals the psychological horror hiding in family dynamics.
5. **A Head Full of Ghosts** by Paul Tremblay — Reality TV meets possible possession, with an unreliable narrator who keeps the reader perpetually uncertain.

## Themes That Work
- **The Fragility of Identity**: How little it takes to destabilize someone's sense of self.
- **Trust and Betrayal**: The horror of discovering that the people closest to you are not what they seem.
- **Trauma as a Living Thing**: Past wounds that refuse to stay buried and actively shape present reality.
- **The Stories We Tell Ourselves**: How narrative and self-deception construct our experience of reality.
- **Control and Its Illusion**: The terrifying discovery that you are not in control of your own mind or life.

## Common Pitfalls
- **"It was all in their head" as a cheap resolution**: If the entire horror is dismissed as hallucination or dream, the reader feels cheated. The ambiguity must be meaningful, not a cop-out.
- **Confusing the reader instead of the protagonist**: Disorientation should be a controlled effect, not the result of sloppy plotting. The author must know exactly what is real even if the reader does not.
- **Stigmatizing mental illness**: Using mental illness as a plot twist or reducing complex conditions to "the crazy character" undermines the story and alienates readers. The protagonist's vulnerability should evoke empathy, not othering.
