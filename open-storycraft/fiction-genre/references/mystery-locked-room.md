# Locked Room

**Definition**: A mystery centered on an apparently impossible crime, typically a murder committed in a sealed space with no visible means of entry or exit, where the central puzzle is not who did it but how it was done.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **The Impossible Crime**: A crime that appears to defy physical possibility, most classically a murder in a locked room with no way in or out.
- **The Sealed Environment**: A clearly defined, verifiably sealed space (locked room, sealed building, isolated island, snowbound house) that rules out obvious explanations.
- **The Howdunit Puzzle**: The central question shifts from "who" to "how"; the method is the mystery.
- **Logical Solution**: The impossible crime must have a rational, physical explanation that the reader could theoretically deduce from the clues provided.
- **The Elimination Process**: The detective systematically rules out impossible explanations until only the truth remains.

## Optional Tropes
- **The Lecture**: A scene where the detective (or another character) catalogs known methods of committing locked-room crimes before identifying the specific one used here.
- **Misdirection by Timing**: The crime did not actually occur when everyone assumes it did; the locked room is an illusion created by manipulating the timeline.
- **Mechanical Ingenuity**: The solution involves a clever physical mechanism (string, ice, spring-loaded device) that sealed the room after the killer left.
- **The Suicide Theory**: Characters propose the victim killed themselves, which the detective must disprove.
- **Multiple Impossibilities**: More than one impossible event occurs, and solving one unlocks the other.
- **The Armchair Detective**: A brilliant but sedentary investigator who solves the puzzle through pure reasoning rather than legwork.
- **Challenge to the Reader**: An explicit or implicit dare to solve the puzzle before the detective does.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: An intellectual puzzle that seems genuinely impossible, followed by a solution that makes the reader say "of course."
- **Resolution**: The method is fully explained with a logical, satisfying solution that accounts for all evidence; the killer is identified as a consequence of solving the how.
- **Pacing**: Methodical and cerebral; the pleasure is in watching theories be constructed and demolished.
- **Tone**: Intellectual and playful, ranging from academic to suspenseful depending on the author.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The moment the impossible becomes inevitable, when the solution reframes everything the reader thought they knew.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: The impossible situation is established quickly and dramatically, usually within the first two chapters.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,500-4,500 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Occasional; chapters more often end with a theory disproven or a new constraint added to the puzzle.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act (impossible crime established / theories tested and eliminated / solution revealed).

## Comp Titles
1. **The Hollow Man** by John Dickson Carr — Contains the famous "locked room lecture" and remains the definitive impossible crime novel.
2. **The Decagon House Murders** by Yukito Ayatsuji — A Japanese honkaku mystery that revitalized the locked-room form with a devastating twist.
3. **And Then There Were None** by Agatha Christie — Ten people on an isolated island die one by one with no possible killer among them.
4. **The 7 1/2 Deaths of Evelyn Hardcastle** by Stuart Turton — A modern impossible-crime novel that uses genre-blending to reinvent the locked-room puzzle.
5. **An Unwanted Guest** by Shari Lapena — A snowbound hotel where guests are murdered one by one, updating the sealed-environment mystery for contemporary readers.

## Themes That Work
- **Reason conquers the impossible**: The locked room represents chaos and irrationality; solving it reasserts that the world is knowable.
- **Appearances deceive**: What looks impossible is merely misunderstood; every locked room is unlocked once you see it correctly.
- **The limits of observation**: Witnesses see what they expect to see, not what actually happened; the solution exploits this gap.
- **Hubris of the criminal**: The elaborate method required to create the impossibility is also the killer's vulnerability.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Solution too simple**: If the answer is "there was a hidden door no one checked," the reader feels cheated; the solution must be clever enough to justify the impossibility.
- **Solution too complex**: If the method requires twelve steps, three accomplices, and technology that barely exists, the reader cannot believe it would work in practice.
- **Puzzle without characters**: If the people in the locked room are cardboard, the reader solves the puzzle but does not care about the answer; even in the most cerebral mysteries, someone must matter.
