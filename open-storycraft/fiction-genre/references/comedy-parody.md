# Parody / Satire

**Definition**: Fiction that mocks, exaggerates, or subverts the conventions of a specific genre, institution, or cultural phenomenon for comedic effect.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Genre Awareness**: Characters or narrative voice demonstrate knowledge of the conventions being mocked, either explicitly or through exaggerated adherence.
- **Escalating Absurdity**: The parody pushes its target's logic to extremes, revealing the inherent ridiculousness of the source material.
- **Subverted Expectations**: Familiar tropes are set up and then deliberately broken, twisted, or taken to their logical (and hilarious) conclusion.
- **Satirical Target**: There is a clear, identifiable thing being skewered, whether a genre, a social institution, a political system, or a cultural trend.

## Optional Tropes
- **Pastiche Characters**: Archetypes from the target genre appear in exaggerated or inverted form.
- **Breaking the Fourth Wall**: The narrator or characters acknowledge the audience, the medium, or the absurdity of their own existence.
- **Deadpan Delivery**: The most ridiculous events are described in flat, serious prose, amplifying the comedy through contrast.
- **Running Gags**: Recurring jokes that escalate or transform with each appearance.
- **Unreliable Narrator**: The narrator's perspective is itself part of the joke.
- **Lampshade Hanging**: Characters explicitly point out the absurdity of genre conventions they are trapped in.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: You will laugh at something you love (or hate) by seeing it reflected in a funhouse mirror.
- **Resolution**: The satirical point lands clearly, and the story either collapses its own premise in a satisfying way or circles back to affirm what the target genre actually does well.
- **Pacing**: Fast, with jokes per page as a key metric. Slower sections risk losing the comedic thread.
- **Tone**: Sharp and knowing. Can range from affectionate ribbing to vicious takedown.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The feeling of recognition. The reader's own genre literacy becomes part of the joke.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Immediate. The first page should signal what is being parodied and establish comedic tone within the first scene.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,000-4,000 words. Short chapters keep the pace brisk and allow for rapid tonal shifts.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Most chapters end on a punchline or an absurd escalation rather than a traditional cliffhanger.
- **Act Structure**: Loose three-act, often mirroring the target genre's structure while subverting it.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy** by Douglas Adams — The gold standard of sci-fi parody, mocking bureaucracy, philosophy, and space opera conventions with deadpan absurdity.
2. **Bored of the Rings** by Harvard Lampoon — Direct Tolkien parody that exaggerates every epic fantasy trope to breaking point.
3. **Guards! Guards!** by Terry Pratchett — Affectionate fantasy parody that uses genre mockery as a vehicle for genuine social commentary.
4. **Redshirts** by John Scalzi — Star Trek parody where expendable crew members realize they are expendable crew members.
5. **Don Quixote** by Miguel de Cervantes — The original genre parody, mocking chivalric romance through a deluded knight who takes fiction literally.

## Themes That Work
- **Institutional Absurdity**: Bureaucracies, militaries, and governments are inherently funny when examined closely. Parody reveals that the systems we take seriously are often held together by habit and delusion.
- **Genre as Mirror**: What a genre chooses to romanticize, ignore, or exaggerate reveals cultural assumptions. Good parody makes those assumptions visible.
- **The Gap Between Ideal and Real**: Heroes who are incompetent, chosen ones who are unwilling, prophecies that are mistranslated. The distance between what should be and what is generates endless comedy.
- **Power and Its Pretensions**: Authority figures who are clearly winging it. Systems that demand reverence but deserve none.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Punching Down at the Audience**: If the parody mocks readers for loving the genre rather than celebrating the genre's absurdities alongside them, it alienates its core audience. The best parodies are written by people who clearly love the thing they are mocking.
- **Reference Without Joke**: Merely pointing at a trope is not humor. "Look, it is a chosen one!" is recognition, not comedy. The parody must do something with the reference: twist it, escalate it, or invert it.
- **No Story Underneath**: If the parody is only jokes, it collapses. There needs to be an actual narrative with stakes, even if those stakes are absurd. Readers need a reason to keep turning pages beyond the next punchline.
