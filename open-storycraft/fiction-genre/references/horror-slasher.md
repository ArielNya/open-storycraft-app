# Slasher

**Definition**: Horror driven by a relentless killer who stalks and murders a group of victims, typically in an isolated setting, with escalating tension leading to a final confrontation between the killer and the last survivor.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **The Killer**: A distinct antagonist (masked, deformed, supernatural, or otherwise iconic) who hunts victims with purpose or compulsion.
- **High Body Count**: Multiple characters die over the course of the story, often in varied and memorable ways.
- **The Final Survivor**: One character (traditionally the "final girl" but not exclusively female) outlasts the others and confronts the killer directly.
- **Isolation**: The group is cut off from help, whether by geography, circumstance, or the killer's preparation.
- **The Hunt**: Extended sequences of pursuit, hiding, and narrow escapes that generate sustained tension.

## Optional Tropes
- **Transgression as Target**: Characters who break rules (social, moral, or survival-based) are killed first or most gruesomely.
- **The Killer's Origin**: A traumatic backstory, past injustice, or supernatural event that created the killer.
- **Ensemble Cast**: A diverse group of distinct personalities, each providing a different reaction to the threat.
- **Red Herrings**: False suspects or misleading clues about the killer's identity.
- **Unmasking**: The killer's identity is revealed in a climactic moment that recontextualizes earlier events.
- **The False Death**: The killer appears defeated but returns for one final attack.
- **Survival Rules**: Characters articulate (implicitly or explicitly) the "rules" for staying alive.
- **Trophy or Signature**: The killer has a distinctive weapon, method, or calling card.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: A propulsive, body-count-driven thriller where death is always one page away and the tension never lets up.
- **Resolution**: The final survivor defeats or escapes the killer, often through cleverness and will rather than strength. The killer may or may not be truly dead.
- **Pacing**: Fast after the first kill, with brief character beats between escalating set pieces.
- **Tone**: Tense, visceral, often darkly fun with moments of gallows humor.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The thrill of the chase, the variety and creativity of the kills, and the catharsis of the final survivor's victory.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Fast. Establish the setting and ensemble quickly. The first kill should come early (within the first 15-20%) to set the stakes.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,000-3,500 words. Short, punchy chapters that mirror the breathless pace.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Every chapter or nearly so. Someone is always in danger.
- **Act Structure**: Countdown model. Act 1: ensemble assembled, isolation established, first kill. Act 2: body count rises, group fractures, attempts to fight back or escape fail. Act 3: final survivor confronts the killer.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Final Girl Support Group** by Grady Hendrix — A meta-slasher where final girls from past massacres are targeted by a new killer.
2. **There's Someone Inside Your House** by Stephanie Perkins — A masked killer targets high school students, unmasking their secrets before killing them.
3. **My Heart Is a Chainsaw** by Stephen Graham Jones — A slasher-obsessed protagonist recognizes the pattern unfolding in her town and tries to survive it.
4. **The Last Final Girl** by Stephen Graham Jones — A self-aware slasher narrative that interrogates and celebrates the genre's conventions.
5. **Clown in a Cornfield** by Adam Cesare — A small-town setting where a killer in a clown mask targets teenagers, blending slasher thrills with social commentary.

## Themes That Work
- **Who Deserves to Survive**: The slasher's implicit moral framework about transgression and punishment, and whether the narrative endorses or interrogates it.
- **Community and Its Discontents**: The killer often emerges from a community's buried guilt, injustice, or hypocrisy.
- **The Resourcefulness of the Underestimated**: The final survivor is typically someone dismissed or overlooked who proves to be the most capable under pressure.
- **Violence as Spectacle**: The genre's relationship with the audience's desire to witness violence, which the best slashers interrogate rather than simply indulge.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Disposable characters**: If the reader does not care about anyone who dies, the kills have no weight. Each victim needs enough personality and connection to make their death register emotionally, even briefly.
- **Killer without coherence**: The killer needs internal logic, whether psychological, supernatural, or thematic. Random violence without pattern or motivation is boring, not scary.
- **Final survivor by default**: The last character standing must earn their survival through action, intelligence, or will. If they survive simply because the plot needs someone alive at the end, the climax falls flat.
