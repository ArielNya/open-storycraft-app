# Gaslamp Fantasy

**Definition**: Fantasy set in a Victorian, Edwardian, or early industrial-era setting where magic coexists with gaslight, steam, corsets, and scientific advancement.

## Mandatory Tropes

- **Historical-Era Setting**: 19th or early 20th century aesthetics — gas lamps, carriages, formal social structures.
- **Magic + Technology**: Both exist. Magic hasn't replaced technology; they operate alongside each other.
- **Social Hierarchy**: Class, gender, and social convention are active forces in the plot.
- **Atmosphere**: The fog, the cobblestones, the drawing rooms. Setting is experiential.

## Optional Tropes

- **Secret Societies**: Magical organizations operating within polite society.
- **Supernatural Mystery**: Investigation of magical crimes using period-appropriate methods.
- **Manners and Magic**: Social etiquette as a form of power, complicated by supernatural abilities.
- **Steampunk Elements**: Mechanical inventions, clockwork, steam-powered devices.
- **Female Protagonist Breaking Conventions**: A woman navigating a restrictive society with magic as her edge.
- **Gothic Elements**: Haunted manors, dark secrets, atmospheric dread.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: The elegance and drama of a period setting, supercharged with magic.
- **Resolution**: The mystery is solved, the social order is disrupted or restored, the protagonist claims their place.
- **Pacing**: Moderate. Social maneuvering and atmosphere need room to breathe.
- **Tone**: Elegant but not stuffy. Wit expected.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The aesthetic pleasure of the period combined with the thrill of magic breaking the rules.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establish the period setting and introduce the magical element within the first chapter.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-4,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Moderate. Social revelations and magical revelations both serve as hooks.
- **Act Structure**: 3-act, often mystery-structured.

## Comp Titles

1. **Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell** by Susanna Clarke — Definitive gaslamp. Magic returns to Regency England.
2. **Sorcerer to the Crown** by Zen Cho — Regency England, magical society, social comedy.
3. **The Invisible Library** by Genevieve Cogman — Victorian-adjacent, librarian protagonist, multiverse.
4. **A Darker Shade of Magic** by V.E. Schwab — Multiple Londons, one Georgian-era, magic as commodity.
5. **Gail Carriger's Parasol Protectorate** — Victorian London with vampires, werewolves, and manners.

## Themes That Work
- **Progress vs. tradition**: Magic as the old way, science as the new, and the tension between them.
- **Social constraint and liberation**: A protagonist who uses magic to break free from period limitations.
- **The hidden world**: What's really happening behind the respectable facade.
- **Empire and its costs**: Using the historical period to examine colonialism and power.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Steampunk cosplay, not story**: All aesthetic, no substance. The period setting must create meaningful constraints and conflicts, not just be pretty wallpaper.
- **Modern protagonist in a costume**: Characters who have 21st-century sensibilities without earning them through the story. The period's limitations should be genuinely felt.
- **Magic makes class irrelevant**: If magic erases all social barriers, you've lost the tension the period provides.
