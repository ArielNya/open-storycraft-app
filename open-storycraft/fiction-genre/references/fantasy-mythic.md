# Mythic Fantasy

**Definition**: Fantasy that draws directly from real-world mythology, folklore, or legend, retelling, reinterpreting, or extending existing mythic traditions.

## Mandatory Tropes

- **Mythological Source**: A recognizable mythological tradition (Greek, Norse, Celtic, Hindu, Yoruba, Japanese, etc.) as foundation.
- **Mythic Scope**: The story operates on a larger-than-human scale — gods, titans, creation, apocalypse.
- **Archetypal Characters**: Characters who embody mythic roles (the trickster, the mother, the warrior, the sacrifice).
- **Thematic Weight**: Mythic fantasy addresses fundamental human questions — mortality, divine justice, fate vs. free will.

## Optional Tropes

- **Gods as Characters**: Deities with personalities, agendas, and flaws.
- **Retelling**: A known myth retold from a new perspective (the monster's POV, the forgotten sister).
- **Mythic Journey**: A hero's journey that follows the mythic pattern deliberately.
- **Mythic Creatures**: Dragons, sphinxes, kitsune, thunderbirds — drawn from real folklore.
- **Cultural Specificity**: Deep research into a specific tradition rather than generic "mythology."
- **Mythic Language**: Prose style that echoes oral storytelling, poetic, rhythmic.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: The power and resonance of myths you know (or want to know), brought to life with modern storytelling craft.
- **Resolution**: The mythic cycle completes. The hero transforms. The prophecy is fulfilled or subverted.
- **Pacing**: Moderate. Mythic stories breathe; they're not thrillers.
- **Tone**: Elevated, resonant. Not casual.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The feeling of touching something ancient and eternal through story.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establish the mythic world and its rules. Can use elevated language.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Low to moderate.
- **Act Structure**: Often follows mythic structure (departure, initiation, return).

## Content Heat Map
| Dimension | Level (1-5) | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| Violence  | 3-4 | Myths are violent. Heroes die, monsters dismember. |
| Romance   | 2-4 | Mythic love stories can be central or absent |
| Language   | 2 | Tends toward elevated register |
| Darkness  | 3-4 | Myths don't flinch from tragedy, sacrifice, or horror |

## Comp Titles

1. **Circe** by Madeline Miller — Greek mythology retelling from the witch of Aiaia's perspective.
2. **The Song of Achilles** by Madeline Miller — The Iliad reimagined as a love story.
3. **American Gods** by Neil Gaiman — Old gods vs. new gods in modern America.
4. **The Poppy War** by R.F. Kuang — Chinese mythology woven into a grimdark war story.
5. **Black Leopard, Red Wolf** by Marlon James — African mythology as literary fantasy.

## Themes That Work
- **Fate vs. free will**: Can you escape what the gods have decreed?
- **The cost of immortality**: What do the undying lose that mortals possess?
- **Divine injustice**: Gods who are powerful but not good.
- **The stories we tell ourselves**: Myth as the lens through which cultures understand reality.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Surface-level appropriation**: Using mythological names and creatures without understanding their cultural significance. Research deeply.
- **Greek-only syndrome**: Greek mythology is overrepresented. Other traditions (West African, Mesoamerican, Polynesian, Hindu) are rich and underexplored.
- **Modern values imposed**: Characters in mythic settings who think and act like 21st-century people. Let the mythic worldview be genuinely different.
