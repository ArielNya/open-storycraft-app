# Fantasy Romance

**Definition**: Romance set in a secondary or magical world where the fantasy plot and the love story carry equal narrative weight, with neither subordinate to the other.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **HEA/HFN**: The couple must achieve a happily ever after or happy for now ending.
- **Fantasy World as Crucible**: The magical or fantastical setting must create unique obstacles and stakes for the romance that could not exist in a mundane world.
- **Dual Engine Plot**: Both the fantasy arc (quest, war, political intrigue) and the romance arc must have their own escalation and climax.
- **Earned Power Dynamic**: If one partner holds magical or political power, the relationship must evolve toward mutual respect and equity.

## Optional Tropes
- **Enemies to Lovers**: Opposing sides of a war, rival courts, or political enemies forced together.
- **Chosen One + Protector**: A destined hero paired with the warrior or advisor sworn to guard them.
- **Forced Proximity**: Quests, sieges, or magical bonds that trap the couple together.
- **Forbidden Magic**: The relationship itself carries a magical cost or is outlawed.
- **Bargain/Debt**: One character owes the other a magical favor that evolves into something real.
- **Court Intrigue**: Romance navigated through political marriage, alliances, and betrayal.
- **Touch Her and Die**: A lethally protective love interest whose violence is reserved for threats to the partner.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: An epic love story set against a world worth exploring, where the romance and the adventure amplify each other.
- **Resolution**: Both the external fantasy conflict and the romantic conflict resolve in the same climax or within pages of each other.
- **Pacing**: Moderate to fast. The fantasy plot provides action beats between romantic development.
- **Tone**: Ranges from lush and romantic to gritty and high-stakes. Often emotionally intense.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The sense that the couple saved each other and the world, and that neither victory was possible without the relationship.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Moderate to fast. Establish the world and the charged dynamic between the leads within the first three chapters.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-5,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Most chapters. Alternating romantic tension beats with adventure/danger beats keeps momentum.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act or four-act. Fantasy romance often runs longer (100k-150k words) to service both plot engines. Series are common, with each book featuring a different couple or deepening one relationship across the arc.

## Comp Titles
1. **A Court of Thorns and Roses** by Ana J. Maas — Fae courts, escalating power, and a romance that reshapes the heroine.
2. **From Blood and Ash** by Jennifer L. Armentrout — Chosen one mythology fused with a slow-burn romance and world-ending stakes.
3. **The Bridge Kingdom** by Danielle L. Jensen — Enemies-to-lovers political marriage where both leads hold real power.
4. **Daughter of the Moon Goddess** by Sue Lynn Tan — East Asian mythology with a quest narrative and a tender, restrained romance.
5. **Radiance** by Grace Draven — A marriage-of-convenience between species where genuine affection grows from mutual respect.

## Themes That Work
- **Love as Transformation**: Both characters become stronger, wiser, or more whole through the relationship.
- **Power and Vulnerability**: Letting someone past your defenses when the world demands you keep them up.
- **Duty vs. Desire**: Choosing between political obligation and personal happiness, then finding a way to honor both.
- **Found Family**: The couple builds or joins a chosen community that becomes home.
- **Sacrifice and Its Limits**: How much are you willing to lose for love, and when does sacrifice become self-destruction?

## Common Pitfalls
- **Romance Swallows the Fantasy**: If the world-building and external plot are afterthoughts, this becomes paranormal romance in a fantasy skin. Both engines must pull their weight.
- **Chosen One Passivity**: The romantically pursued character must have their own goals, agency, and competence beyond being desired.
- **Stakes Without Cost**: If the fantasy conflict never actually threatens the romance (characters never face real loss or hard choices), the tension is hollow.
