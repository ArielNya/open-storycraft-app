# Romantic Fantasy

**Definition**: Fantasy where the romance is co-equal to (or drives) the plot, set in a fully realized fantasy world with magic, politics, and stakes beyond the relationship.

## Mandatory Tropes

- **Central Romance**: The love story is the A-plot or shares equal weight with the fantasy plot.
- **Fantasy World**: A genuine secondary-world or urban-fantasy setting, not just a backdrop.
- **HEA or HFN**: Happily Ever After or Happy For Now is expected (borrows from romance genre contract).
- **Emotional Intensity**: The relationship is passionate, complex, and drives character decisions.

## Optional Tropes

- **Fated Mates**: Magical bond or destiny connecting the lovers.
- **Enemies to Lovers**: Political, magical, or factional opposition that becomes attraction.
- **Forbidden Love**: Cross-faction, cross-species, or power-differential relationships.
- **Training/Academy**: Learning magic together creates proximity and tension.
- **Dual POV**: Alternating perspectives between the love interests.
- **Magic-Bonded**: A spell, curse, or magical circumstance that forces proximity.
- **Slow Burn**: Extended tension before the relationship is acknowledged.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: An epic love story set in a world worth exploring, where both the romance and the fantasy deliver.
- **Resolution**: The couple ends up together. The fantasy threat is resolved. Both arcs are satisfying.
- **Pacing**: Moderate to fast. Romance beats should interleave with fantasy action/politics.
- **Tone**: Passionate, dramatic. High emotional register.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The moment the couple finally gets together, combined with a fantasy world that earns its own attention.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Establish both the fantasy world and the romantic dynamic in the first 2-3 chapters.
- **Chapter Length**: 3,000-4,000 words.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: High. Both romantic tension and plot tension drive chapter hooks.
- **Act Structure**: 3-act. The romance arc and the fantasy arc should have parallel turning points.

## Comp Titles

1. **A Court of Thorns and Roses** by Ana J. Maas — The genre-defining modern romantic fantasy series.
2. **From Blood and Ash** by Jennifer L. Armentrout — Romantic fantasy with paranormal elements, action, and heat.
3. **Fourth Wing** by Rebecca Yarros — Military academy, dragon riders, enemies to lovers.
4. **Daughter of the Moon Goddess** by Sue Lynn Tan — Chinese mythology-inspired romantic fantasy.
5. **The Bridge Kingdom** by Danielle L. Jensen — Enemies to lovers, political marriage, intrigue.

## Themes That Work
- **Vulnerability as strength**: Opening yourself to love in a world that punishes weakness.
- **Power dynamics in relationships**: When one partner has magic/political/military power the other doesn't.
- **Love vs. duty**: Choosing the person or choosing the kingdom/mission/faction.
- **Healing through connection**: Partners who heal each other's wounds (emotional, magical, or literal).

## Common Pitfalls
- **Fantasy world as wallpaper**: If the fantasy elements could be removed without changing the story, the "fantasy" part isn't doing its job. The world must create unique obstacles for the romance.
- **Romance overshadows plot**: Readers signed up for BOTH. If the fantasy plot dissolves into just a vehicle for romantic scenes, half the promise is broken.
- **Insta-love without earning it**: Fated mates is a trope, not an excuse to skip character development. The reader needs to fall in love with both characters.
