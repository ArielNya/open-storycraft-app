# Action-Adventure

**Definition**: Fiction driven by relentless physical momentum where fights, chases, explosions, and set pieces are the primary narrative engine, and the protagonist survives through skill, grit, and improvisation.

## Mandatory Tropes
- **Set Piece Sequences**: Large-scale action scenes that are elaborately constructed, visually vivid, and serve as the structural pillars of the story.
- **Competent Protagonist**: The hero is skilled at something relevant to survival. They may be outmatched, but they are never helpless.
- **Escalating Threats**: Each obstacle is bigger, more dangerous, or more complex than the last. The stakes ratchet upward continuously.
- **Physical Stakes**: The danger is concrete and bodily. Someone could die, get hurt, or lose something tangible in every major scene.
- **Ticking Clock**: Time pressure drives decisions. There is always a deadline, a countdown, or a closing window.

## Optional Tropes
- **The Reluctant Hero**: Drawn into the conflict against their will but proving to be exactly the right person for the job.
- **The Villain With Resources**: An antagonist with superior numbers, technology, or position, forcing the hero to be creative.
- **The Sidekick or Partner**: A companion who complements the hero's skills and provides someone to talk to during downtime.
- **Globe-Trotting Locations**: Multiple exotic or dramatic settings that provide visual variety for action sequences.
- **Vehicle Sequences**: Chases involving cars, boats, planes, horses, or spacecraft that use the vehicle itself as a story element.
- **The Training or Preparation Montage**: A sequence where the hero gears up, plans, or trains for the coming confrontation.
- **Betrayal**: An ally revealed as an enemy, raising the stakes and isolating the hero.
- **The Final Confrontation**: A climactic one-on-one showdown between hero and villain.

## Reader Expectations
- **Promise**: Nonstop forward momentum. You will not be bored.
- **Resolution**: The hero wins, though possibly at a cost. The threat is neutralized. Justice or survival is achieved.
- **Pacing**: Fast. Scenes move, chapters end on hooks, and downtime is brief and purposeful.
- **Tone**: High energy, serious stakes, but with room for quips and camaraderie.
- **Satisfaction Source**: The visceral thrill of watching a skilled person overcome impossible odds through action.

## Pacing Conventions
- **Opening**: Start with action or its immediate aftermath. The first chapter should establish the protagonist's capability and the story's kinetic energy.
- **Chapter Length**: 2,500-4,000 words. Short enough to maintain urgency.
- **Cliffhanger Frequency**: Every chapter or nearly so. The reader should always feel compelled to continue.
- **Act Structure**: Three-act. Act 1 establishes hero and threat. Act 2 escalates through set pieces. Act 3 delivers the climactic confrontation.

## Comp Titles
1. **The Bourne Identity** by Robert Ludlum — An amnesiac assassin piecing together his identity while outrunning everyone who wants him dead. The template for modern action-adventure.
2. **Clive Cussler's Sahara** by Clive Cussler — Dirk Pitt adventures across the desert combining historical mystery with relentless action set pieces.
3. **Ready Player One** by Ernest Cline — Action-adventure in a virtual world with puzzles, chases, and escalating confrontations.
4. **The Lost Symbol** by Dan Brown — Puzzle-solving under extreme time pressure with chase sequences through real-world locations.
5. **Ice Station** by Matthew Reilly — A military team trapped in an Antarctic base with enemies closing in from all sides, pure kinetic storytelling.

## Themes That Work
- **Competence Under Pressure**: The satisfaction of watching someone who is good at what they do pushed to their absolute limit and finding a way through.
- **The Cost of Violence**: Action without consequence is hollow. The best action-adventure acknowledges what fighting takes from the people who do it.
- **Loyalty and Sacrifice**: Who you trust when everything goes wrong, and what you are willing to give up to protect them.
- **Resourcefulness Over Power**: The hero rarely has the biggest gun. They win through cleverness, improvisation, and refusing to quit.

## Common Pitfalls
- **Action Without Stakes**: If the reader never believes the hero could lose, the action is just choreography. There must be real danger, real cost, and real consequences for failure.
- **Identical Set Pieces**: Each action sequence must be distinct in setting, challenge, and what it demands from the hero. Three gunfights in a row are one gunfight repeated.
- **Cardboard Characters**: If the reader does not care about the person in danger, the danger means nothing. Even in the most action-heavy stories, character work in the quiet moments is what makes the loud moments matter.
