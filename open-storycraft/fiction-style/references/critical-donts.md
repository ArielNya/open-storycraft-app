# Critical DON'Ts

These rules are universal. Apply to every story.

---

## Forbidden Phrases (AI-isms)

These patterns mark amateur or AI-generated prose. Never use:

- "practiced precision" / "practiced ease" / "practiced [anything]"
- "a mix of [X] and [Y]" / "a mixture of" / "a combination of"
- "couldn't help but [verb]"
- "found himself [verbing]" / "found herself [verbing]"
- "something akin to"
- "a sense of"
- "not [X] but [Y]" / "it wasn't [X], it was [Y]" (paradiastole)
- "the kind of [X] that..." (e.g., "the kind of smile that...")
- "quiet efficiency"
- "with a knowing [noun]"
- "seemed to [verb]" when you mean they did the verb
- "symphony of [action]" / "dance of [action]" / "ballet of [action]"

---

## Not-Yet-Veterans Rule

Young characters (under 30) should not move or act like trained operatives unless they ARE trained operatives. Forbidden descriptors for inexperienced characters:

- "practiced" / "practiced ease"
- "seasoned"
- "veteran"
- "clinical" (for movements)
- "steely" (for expressions)
- "with efficiency"
- "methodically" (unless established as trained)

**Why**: A 16-year-old doesn't have "practiced calm." They're faking it, or they're panicking. Show the gap between what they're trying to project and what they're actually feeling.

## Forbidden Words

- cacophony
- symphony (unless literal music)
- rhythm (metaphorical - "rhythm of the city")
- verdant
- tapestry/tapestries (metaphorical)
- testament
- sentinel
- cerulean
- whisper/whispers/whispering (unless actual speech volume)
- "sun kissed the horizon"
- palpable
- tangible (for emotions)
- mellifluous
- literally (filler word)
- quite (softener - commit or cut)
- rather (softener - commit or cut)

## Punctuation Rules

### EM DASHES
- **ONLY** use when a character's speech, thought, or internal monologue is interrupted
- Dialogue interruption: "I was just—" "No."
- Thought interruption: She could not form a complete— Oh god.
- **NEVER** use em dashes in narrative description
- Replace narrative em dashes with periods, commas, or colons
- Use commas for brief pauses, periods for full stops

### FRAGMENTS
- **NEVER** use sentence fragments in narrative prose
- The only exception: when a character's thought, monologue, or dialogue is interrupted (use em dash)
- Short complete sentences create urgency without breaking grammar
- "The door was locked." NOT "The door. Locked."
- "She could not think." NOT "Couldn't think."

### SEMICOLONS
- Use sparingly
- Only between closely related independent clauses
- If in doubt, use a period

### ELLIPSIS
- Trailing off in dialogue: "I thought maybe..."
- NOT for dramatic pauses in narrative
- Three dots only, no more

## Rule of Threes (Avoid)

Triadic lists are an AI writing tell. Don't use "X, Y, and Z" for stylistic effect.

**Forbidden**:
- "cold, dark, and empty"
- Three-beat descriptions: "Rusted pipes, dim lighting, the smell of decay."

**Instead**: Use two items, or four+, or rephrase entirely.

**Better**:
- "cold and dark"
- "The pipes had rusted. Something had died in here."

---

## Negation Hedging (Avoid)

Defining something by what it ISN'T instead of what it IS. This is an AI tell: the model
lacks confidence in a positive claim, so it retreats into a negative one that sounds like
prose but contains no information.

**Forbidden**:
- "She didn't say anything." (what did she do?)
- "It wasn't quiet." (what did it sound like?)
- "He didn't know what to think." (what was actually going through his head?)
- "It wasn't the welcome he'd expected." (what was it?)
- "She didn't look angry." (what did she look like?)

**Fix**: Replace every negative with the concrete positive observation. If you can't name
what something IS, you haven't decided yet. Decide, then write it.

**Better**:
- "She looked at the wall."
- "The air sat thick and damp, the kind that clings to everything."
- "His mind kept sliding off the question."

**Exception**: Deliberate negation for irony or contrast is fine when intentional and the
sentence still lands on something ("It wasn't a warning, it was a verdict.") That's a
choice. "She didn't say anything" is not. Also fine: "He didn't move" when the stillness
itself is the point.

## Dialogue Tags

### Use:
- "said" (invisible, preferred)
- "asked"
- Action beats (preferred over tags)
- Occasionally: "muttered," "shouted," "whispered" (volume only)

### Never use:
- "breathed," "murmured," "intoned," "mused"
- "quipped," "retorted," "exclaimed"
- Any tag that describes HOW something was said

### Never personify voices:
- NO: "His voice carried weight"
- NO: "Her voice was velvet"
- YES: "He spoke quietly" (volume)
- YES: "She dropped her voice" (action)

---

## Crutch Words to Eliminate

Search and destroy:
- "just" (unless essential)
- "really" / "very"
- "suddenly"
- "And then" (starting sentences)
- "began to" / "started to" (just do the action)
- "seemed to" (either they did or didn't)
- "that" (often unnecessary: "knew that he" → "knew he")

---

## Adjective Slaughter Rule

Never use more than one physical descriptor per body part per chapter.

**Bad** (same chapter):
> "Her green eyes sparkled." ... later ... "Her emerald eyes narrowed."

**Why**: Once you've described eyes as green, the reader remembers. Repeating with variation ("emerald") feels like padding. And stacking descriptors ("her sparkling emerald eyes") is worse.

**The Rule**:
- One descriptor per body part, period
- If you've said "green eyes" once, just say "eyes" afterward
- Trust the reader's memory
- If you must re-emphasize, find a different angle (what the eyes are DOING, not what color they are)

---

## ASMR Over-Description

Avoid excessive sensory detail that doesn't serve character or plot:

- NO: Lingering on irrelevant textures
- NO: Redundant descriptions of same state
- NO: Decorative language without purpose
- YES: Sensory details that reveal character or advance tension

**Bad**: "She ran her fingers along the smooth, cool marble of the countertop, feeling each vein of grey that threaded through the pristine white surface."

**Good**: "The marble was cold. She steadied herself against the counter."

---

## Simile & Metaphor Limits

- Maximum 2-3 similes per chapter
- Never cluster in same paragraph
- Each must serve character voice
- Prefer direct description over similes

**Ask**: Would THIS character think this comparison?

A mechanic wouldn't think "like a wounded swan." They'd think "like a stripped bolt."
