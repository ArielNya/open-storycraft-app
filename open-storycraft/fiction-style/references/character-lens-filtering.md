# Character-Lens Filtering

Close third person isn't just POV choice - it's a filtering system. Everything on the page passes through the protagonist's brain.

---

## The Core Principle

The narrative IS the character's interior thought process. There is no separation between narrator and character. Their judgments, assumptions, and speculations are integrated directly with sensory perceptions.

**Not this**: A neutral narrator describing what the character perceives.
**This**: The character's brain registering and interpreting reality.

---

## The Formula

**Observation + Reaction + Judgment = Character Voice**

Every description should pass through all three stages:

1. **Observation**: What the character physically perceives
2. **Reaction**: Their immediate emotional/physical response
3. **Judgment**: Their interpretation based on personality, experience, values

### Example Transformation

**Sterile (observation only):**
> Director Vale stood looking out at the Potomac when they entered. She turned to face them. The office was cold.

**Filtered (full character lens):**
> Director Vale was at the window when they entered, looking out at the Potomac. The corner office had a hell of a view - monuments in the distance, river cutting through the city. She'd come a long way from managing HOA complaints. She turned to face them. The whole building was freezing. Rowan wondered if the budget didn't cover heat, or if Director Vale was just used to Boston winters.

The second version shows us Rowan's:
- **Observation**: Window, view, Director Vale turning
- **Reaction**: Noticing the cold, being impressed/skeptical
- **Judgment**: Commentary on her career trajectory, speculation about why it's cold

## What Different Characters Notice

The filter changes based on WHO is perceiving. Their personality determines their attention.

### Suspicious/Tactical Character
- Exits and entrances
- People's hands (weapons, nervousness)
- Inconsistencies in stories
- Who's watching whom
- Escape routes, cover positions

### Romantic/Emotional Character
- How people move
- Small gestures of care
- Tension between people
- Emotional undercurrents
- What people aren't saying

### Practical/Technical Character
- What works and what doesn't
- Resources and tools available
- Efficiency (or lack thereof)
- Problems that need solving
- How things are built/organized

### Young Character (under 25)
- Physical discomfort first (hungry, cold, tired)
- Random specific details (someone's weird haircut)
- Pop culture comparisons
- Whether they're being judged
- Wandering attention even in serious moments

## Internal Monologue Integration

Internal thoughts should be woven INTO the narrative, not separated as italicized asides. The narrative IS the character's internal monologue.

### Wrong (separated)
> The door opened and a man entered. *He looks dangerous*, Rowan thought.

### Right (integrated)
> The door opened. Rowan tracked the man's hands first, then his face. Too confident for a messenger.

The character's interpretation is embedded in the description itself. "Too confident for a messenger" IS Rowan's thought, delivered as observation.

### Impulsive and Raw

Internal monologue should be impulsive, not measured. Characters don't think in polished paragraphs.

**Wrong (too composed)**:
> She considered the implications of his statement. There were several reasons why he might be lying, and she weighed each one carefully.

**Right (raw, immediate)**:
> That was bullshit. She could smell it. He was hiding something.

The character reacts FIRST, analyzes SECOND (if at all). Thoughts are reactive, not essayistic.

### Kinesthetic Emotion

Show emotion through physical sensation, not abstract labels.

**Abstract (avoid)**:
> She felt angry.

**Kinesthetic (use)**:
> Her jaw clenched. Heat crawled up her neck.

The body registers emotion before the mind names it.

## Emotional Coloring

Don't describe objectively. Describe through the character's emotional state.

| Emotional State | Same Room Described |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Neutral | "The room was small." |
| Anxious | "The room pressed in. Too small. Walls too close." |
| Comfortable | "The room felt intimate. Contained." |
| Hostile | "The room was a box. No windows. One door." |

The physical space hasn't changed. The character's perception of it has.

## Practical Assessments

Characters in action stories constantly assess their environment:

### Questions They're Asking
- Where are the exits?
- Who's armed?
- What can be used as a weapon?
- What's the power dynamic in this room?
- Who's the real threat?

### Show This in Description
> The bar had two exits - front door, back through the kitchen. Three guys at the pool table, one watching the door. The bartender kept glancing at something under the counter. Great.

This IS tactical assessment delivered as description.

---

## When Filter Can Lighten

Once you establish the character as the perceiver, you can lighten the filtering slightly to avoid repetitive "Rowan saw" constructions.

**Establishing paragraph:**
> Rowan watched Imani pull out her tablet.

**Subsequent (filter established):**
> She frowned at the screen. Her expression went flat.

We know we're in Rowan's perspective. We don't need to repeat "Rowan saw" for every observation. But we still can't know Imani's thoughts or feelings - only what Rowan observes from her face and behavior.

---

## The Key Test

Before writing any description, ask:
1. Would this character notice this?
2. How would they interpret it?
3. What does this reveal about them?

If the answer to #1 is "no," cut it.
If you can't answer #2 and #3, you're writing camera POV, not close third.
