# Voice & POV Deep Dive

## Narrative Voice Components

### POV Type
- **First Person**: "I walked into the room" - Most intimate, limited to one perspective
- **Third Limited**: "She walked into the room" - Close but allows some distance
- **Third Omniscient**: Can work for chronicle/epic styles with a narrator persona

---

## First-Person Considerations

First person removes the narrator-character separation. The narrative IS the character's voice.

### The Confessional Mode
The character speaks directly to the reader, as if recounting their story:
- Feels like telling a friend over drinks
- Can break fourth wall with asides
- Acknowledges limitations and mistakes
- May admit to guessing or making things up

### Voice Consistency
Everything must sound like this character:
- Their vocabulary, not yours
- Their sentence patterns
- Their blind spots and biases
- Their humor (or lack thereof)

### Knowledge Limitations
First-person narrators only know what they experience or are told:
- They make assumptions (often wrong)
- They speculate about others' motives
- They may lie or omit to the reader
- Unreliable narrator is always an option

---

## Omniscient Narrator with Personality

Not all omniscient narration is distant. The narrator can have a distinct voice.

### The Chronicle Voice
A narrator who "remembers" the events, telling them with investment:
- Warm, paternal, invested in the hero
- Uses loving epithets ("our young hero," "brave Iñigo")
- Interprets actions through a moral/spiritual lens
- Speaks with the gravitas of history

### Show, Then Tell the Meaning
A pattern for omniscient narration:
1. **Act**: State the deed simply
2. **Exhortation**: Explain the virtue demonstrated
3. **Reflection**: Draw a moral conclusion

> "He crossed the river alone, though the men begged him to wait. Such courage is the mark of divine favor; for who trusts in God fears no torrent."

### When Omniscient Works
- Historical/chronicle styles
- Epic fantasy with mythic tone
- Stories where the narrator's perspective is part of the meaning
- When you need access to multiple characters' interiors

### Tense
- **Past Tense**: Most natural for storytelling, allows reflection
- **Present Tense**: More immediate, urgent, but can feel gimmicky if overused

### Narrative Personality
The narrator has a personality even in third person. Options:
- **Wry/Sardonic**: Commentary on events, dry observations
- **Earnest**: Direct emotional engagement, no irony
- **Detached/Clinical**: Observational, lets reader draw conclusions
- **Intimate/Confessional**: Deep interiority, vulnerable

---

## The Core Rule

Distill the narrative voice into one sentence:

**Examples**:
- "The narrative IS the protagonist's internal monologue - their opinions color every description."
- "Clean, precise prose that reveals character through what they notice, not what they think."
- "Urgent, present-tense narration that keeps the reader in constant unease."


## Voice Firewall

Every project needs a negative voice definition as well as a positive one. Identify the kind of prose the protagonist must never sound like.

Common failure modes:
- Analytical or engineer-like: scenes become calculations, systems, variables, data, architecture, geometry, or logic instead of lived pressure.
- Therapist-like: emotions are named, diagnosed, processed, or explained instead of embodied.
- Outline-like: the narration names beats, arcs, inversions, themes, or chapter functions.
- Neutral author voice: descriptions could belong to any narrator, not this character.
- Robotic continuity language: the prose logs information, files reactions, tracks changes, registers signals, or reports states instead of dramatizing them.

For every major motif, define its allowed range. A motif can recur, but it should not become the default syntax for all emotion. If a character counts money under pressure, that does not mean love, shame, grief, and desire should all be written as math.

When revising a robotic sentence, translate abstraction into one of five things:
1. Body: throat, hands, stomach, jaw, breath, heat, pain.
2. Object: bill, cup, knife, door, shoe, phone, steering wheel.
3. Action: moving, freezing, swallowing, putting something down, refusing to look.
4. Silence: what the character does not say.
5. Direct thought: short, biased, imperfect, character-native.

## What the Protagonist Notices

Based on their personality:

### A suspicious character notices:
- Exits and entrances
- People's hands
- Inconsistencies in stories
- Who's watching whom

### A romantic character notices:
- How people move
- Small gestures of care
- Tension between people
- Emotional undercurrents

### A practical character notices:
- What works and what doesn't
- Resources and tools
- Efficiency (or lack thereof)
- Problems that need solving

## Internal Voice Variations

### Calm State
- Longer sentences
- More observation
- Room for reflection
- Complete thoughts

### Stressed State
- Shorter sentences
- Thoughts can be cut off mid-sentence (use em dash for interruption)
- Focus narrows
- Complete sentences, just shorter and more urgent

### Example Contrast

**Calm**: "The market spread out before her, stalls arranged in crooked rows that had probably looked the same for a hundred years. She had time. She could browse."

**Stressed**: "She had taken a wrong turn. The alley was a dead end. Footsteps echoed behind her, getting closer. She ran."

## Reader Relationship

### Close (reader knows everything protagonist knows)
- Deep POV, no information withheld
- Reader experiences discoveries with character
- More emotional immediacy

### Slight Distance (reader suspects more than protagonist)
- Dramatic irony possible
- Reader sees patterns character misses
- More suspense

### Strategic Withholding
- First person narrator can lie or omit
- Creates unreliable narrator effect
- Requires careful handling

---

## Dual Voice Approaches

Some stories require different prose styles for different POV characters.

### Contrasting Voices
When alternating between characters, each can have distinct:
- **Tense**: One character in past, another in present
- **Formality**: Clinical vs. conversational
- **Vocabulary**: Technical vs. accessible
- **Sentence structure**: Complex vs. punchy

### Example: Human vs. AI
- Human sections: Past tense, emotional, sensory language
- AI sections: Present tense, no contractions, precise percentages, clinical

### Marking the Shift
- Chapter or section breaks (never mid-scene)
- Voice shift should be immediately recognizable
- Consistency within each voice is critical

## Language Tone Level

Define the profanity and formality level for the story.

| Level | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Clean** | No profanity, elevated language (chronicle/literary) |
| **Mild** | Occasional "damn" or "hell," otherwise restrained |
| **Moderate** | Natural profanity when appropriate, not excessive |
| **Liberal** | Frequent curse words reflecting character/setting |
| **Crude** | Profanity is part of the character's voice and worldview |

### Integration
Profanity should reflect character, not author:
- A nobleman wouldn't swear like a sailor
- A teenager might overuse it for effect
- Stress increases profanity naturally
- Some cultures/settings have their own expletives
